What a tutor does and does not do
At a glance

A tutor is a volunteer academic staff member who advises, advocates, and signposts — not a subject tutor, not a therapist, not an academic-writing service, and (since the 2025-26 process changes) increasingly not a paperwork-filer. Read this alongside first-contact-and-first-meeting.md for how to convey this to a new tutee.

What a tutor can do (student-facing framing, still broadly accurate)Be a point of contact and confidant, entirely confidentially. Give general educational advice and direction —…
  • Be a point of contact and confidant, entirely confidentially.
  • Give general educational advice and direction — adaptation to university life, not subject-specific help.
  • Assist around academic changes: transfers (advisory only now — see transfers-internal.md), gap year/off-books.
  • Support extension requests where the student prefers not to disclose sensitive matters directly to a marker (see extensions-and-late-submission.md — role has narrowed since Aug 2025).
  • Help interpret exam feedback and, where grounds exist, submit a re-check request (see results-rechecks-and-mark-changes.md).
  • Signpost to services, and make introductions/urgent-appointment requests on the student's behalf where useful (see referrals-student-services.md).
  • Be a confidante and an advocate — including representing the student at a Court of First Appeal and attending an Academic Integrity Meeting.
  • Reach out at the start of each semester (week 6) and before exams.
What a tutor cannot / should not doAnswer instantly — set expectations on response time (a few working days for routine queries). Edit, write,…
  • Answer instantly — set expectations on response time (a few working days for routine queries).
  • Edit, write, critique, or review a student's coursework.
  • Break or bend college rules or regulations — e.g. change a 39% to a 40%.
  • Talk to a student's parents/guardians about their case (see serious-and-urgent-situations.md).
  • Find student accommodation.
  • Provide mental-health support (signpost instead).
  • Speculate on transfer success rates or advise on course career prospects (refer to Careers).
  • Submit Student Cases forms on the student's behalf — this changed in 2025-26; see the conflict note below and each individual process file.
  • Bring an appeal seeking a relief the AAC/CoFA cannot grant, even under pressure from a persistent tutee (see progression-appeals.md).

At a glance

Meet incoming JF tutees one-to-one or as a group during Freshers' Week, using a short scripted introduction; contact your whole chamber again around week 6 of each semester and before exams; all contact goes through your dynamic tutorial chamber mailing list, which updates itself as students join/leave.

Who submits
The tutor introduces themselves and sets expectations; the student decides
Deadlines
  • Freshers' Week meeting: within the first week or two of Michaelmas term.
  • Week-6 contact: Michaelmas and Hilary term, week 6.
  • Pre-exam contact: before each exam period.
  • verify annually for exact Freshers' Week dates each September.

  • Who you are, your department, and (briefly) your own experience as a student.
  • What the tutor does: general educational advice and direction (not subject tutoring); support around exam time (clashes, deferral, feedback); assist with transfers/withdrawal/off-books (⚠️ see conflict note below — this description in the script predates the 2025-26 process change); may request extensions on a student's behalf if they have a medical cert (⚠️ also superseded, see extensions-and-late-submission.md); advocate at academic-integrity or appeal meetings; signposts to services (Disability Service, Counselling, Health, SLD, Careers).

Subject: A couple of supports that might help

Dear [Student name],

Thanks for telling me how things have been going — I wanted to flag a couple of supports that might be useful, entirely at your own pace.

[If mental health / wellbeing] The Student Counselling Service offers free, confidential assessment and short-term counselling, and there are options with no wait: a daily emergency appointment, and drop-in morning triage slots (South Leinster Street, first-come-first-served) if you'd rather not wait for a booked slot. You can self-refer at tcd.ie/studentcounselling or by emailing student-counselling@tcd.ie — or, if you'd find it easier, I'm happy to ring on your behalf: (01) 896 1407.

[If academic skills / adjusting to university-level work] Student Learning Development (SLD) offers free one-to-one appointments, an Academic Writing Centre, and workshops on things like time management, essay writing, and exam prep — self-bookable at tcd.ie/sld. This is often a better first stop than Counselling if the issue is more "I don't know how to structure this essay" than "I'm struggling."

[If disability / ongoing health condition] If you have a diagnosed disability or ongoing condition that's affecting your studies, the Trinity disAbility Service can arrange a Learning Educational Needs Summary (LENS) report, which sets out reasonable accommodations for assessments and exams. You can apply via my.tcd.ie → My Disability Service tab, or email askds@tcd.ie.

None of these commit you to anything beyond a first conversation. Let me know if you'd like me to help set anything up, or just to talk it through first.

Best wishes, [Your Name]

The procedureApplies to undergraduate and taught postgraduate students. Postgraduate research students are out of scope —…

Applies to undergraduate and taught postgraduate students. Postgraduate research students are out of scope — refer directly to the Dean of Graduate Studies.

  1. Module coordinator may arrange an informal meeting to discuss a concern.
  2. If concern remains (or no meeting deemed necessary), the coordinator completes the Academic Integrity Form, generating an indicative score and level.
  3. Level 1: handled within the School by the DUTL.
  4. Levels 2–3: DUTL informed. If the student admits misconduct, a lesser consequence may be offered. If denied, or the DUTL wants further investigation, it proceeds to an Academic Integrity Meeting.
  5. Level 4: referred directly to the Junior Dean.

The Academic Integrity Meeting

  • DUTL (or designate) writes to the student and their tutor, setting out the nature of the suspected misconduct and the evidence, inviting a response via the Academic Integrity Response Form and to attend a meeting. At least 3 days' notice required.
  • Attendees: the DUTL/designate (Chair); two academic colleagues from the School (at least one from the relevant discipline); the student and their tutor (or a PAS/SU representative), if they wish; the module coordinator, if they wish, but only to present additional evidence.
  • The meeting weighs the Academic Integrity Form, the student's Response Form, and verbal submissions; determines the level (if any) and selects a consequence, factoring in mitigating circumstances.
  • Chair completes the Academic Integrity Meeting Decision Form → submitted to the Senior Lecturer for approval, who may approve, reject, or vary the recommendation.
  • Referral to the Junior Dean can also happen where facts are in dispute, regulations were not properly applied, or the DUTL considers the standard consequences inappropriate for the circumstances.

How to lodge an appeal (Court of First Appeal)A tutee wants to appeal a progression decision (e.g. an exclusion) and needs to understand the process and evidence bar

Based on: Tutor Handbook 2025-26; letter to tutors from Registrar (2026-09-03); progression-appeals.md

Subject: Bringing an appeal to the Court of First Appeal

Dear [Student name],

Thanks for letting me know you'd like to appeal. Before we start, it's worth being clear-eyed about what an appeal can and can't do, since the timeline is short and the grounds are specific.

What an appeal is for: it's against the consequences of a decision by your Court of Examiners — for example, an exclusion, or a penalty — never against a mark itself. There are three permitted grounds: your case isn't adequately covered by the ordinary regulations, the regulations weren't properly applied to your case, or genuinely exceptional/compassionate circumstances affected your performance at the time of the assessment (ad misericordiam).

What it can't do: change any mark or degree classification, get you a "special" supplemental exam, let you progress without enough credits, or let you re-attempt work you've already submitted. If your goal falls into one of these, I have to be honest with you now that it isn't something I can bring forward — it would be struck out without a hearing.

Evidence matters enormously. For a health-related ad misericordiam ground, evidence needs to have been obtained at the time of the assessment — evidence obtained well afterwards is not accepted, regardless of how serious the underlying issue was.

Timeline: submission is usually only 1–3 days before the Court of First Appeal date for your Faculty/School — so please get me your account and evidence as early as possible.

Process: you complete Section A of the appeal form; we complete Section B together — I'll help you set it out clearly. I (or the Senior Tutor) will attend the Court of First Appeal to represent you; you're welcome to attend too. Please download your full academic history from my.tcd.ie for me rather than filling in the academic-history table by hand.

Let's find a time to go through your account and gather what we need.

Best wishes, [Your Name]

  1. Ordinary Regulations — the student's case is not adequately covered by College's ordinary regulations.
  2. Improper Application — College regulations were not properly applied in the student's case.

An appeal cannot change the mark given to a piece of work — but it can change the consequences of that mark (e.g. permission to repeat, lifting an exclusion, removing a late-submission penalty).

  • Progression into the next year without sufficient credits to rise.
  • Altering assessment modalities of a module (e.g. directing an exam-based module be assessed by essay instead).

There are two different things people mean by "off-books":

OBN (off-books, no assessment) — a full year off, with no assessments. You're not a registered student that year: no fee, but also no IT account, no Blackboard, no library access, no Trinity services, no ID card. When you come back, you repeat the year in full from September. Note: as a new JF student, you generally can't go OBN before 31 January — if you can't continue right now, withdrawing and reapplying via CAO would be the alternative route.

OBA (off-books, taking assessment) — you repeat up to 3 failed modules (no more than 20 ECTS total) without attending classes, for a fee of €382. You keep your IT account, Blackboard access, and a limited library ticket. This counts as a repeat year. It isn't automatic — it depends on how many credits you've failed and the specifics of your case.

Whichever direction you're leaning, this affects your fees and timeline in ways worth mapping out properly, and there's a form to complete on ask.tcd.ie (Staff & Student Forms → Academic Registry → Student Cases → Offbooks form) with me or the Senior Tutor as your approver.

Let's talk before you submit anything, so we can be sure it's the right fit for your situation.
