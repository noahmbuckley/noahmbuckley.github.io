1. AI and your work

What AI is actually good at, for you

At this level: summarising a dense article before you commit to reading it properly, checking whether your citation format is consistent, or stress-testing an argument you’ve already built. What it can’t do: decide whether your argument about, say, Hungary’s democratic backsliding is any good. That’s still you.

Third-years have usually already formed AI habits by now — this lands better as “here’s how to use it well” than as an intro-101 explainer. Use early in the term, before the mid-term essay brief goes out.

Noah: swap the example for something from the actual reading list that week — Hungary is a placeholder.

The essay isn’t the deliverable, the argument is

A polished 2,500 words that doesn’t commit to a claim is worth less than a rough 2,000 that does. AI is very good at the polish. It cannot decide what you think about whether Orbán’s Hungary is still a democracy — and that decision is the entire assignment.

Best right before or just after the mid-term essay brief lands. This is the framing slide — put it ahead of the “where the line is” slide below.

Where the line actually is

Check the brief for this specific assessment, not your instinct and not what worked in another module. Different assessments in this department allow different things. When in doubt, ask.

Deliberately generic so it doesn’t need updating as policy shifts. Use once, near the mid-term essay brief.

2. How university works

How to email a professor

Bad: “hi wondering if you could go over my essay plan asap.” Good: a subject line that says what it’s about, your name and module code, one clear question, and a sign-off. By third year this should be automatic — it usually isn’t yet.

Even third-years get this wrong more often than you’d expect, especially before the mid-term essay. Use early, ideally before the essay-planning period.

Noah: a real (anonymised) bad example lands harder than an invented one — swap in if you have one you’re comfortable using.

What office hours are for, at this stage

Not “I don’t understand the reading” — by now, come with a claim you’re not sure holds up, or a source you think undercuts your own argument. Office hours get more useful the more formed your thinking already is, not less.

Different pitch than a first-year version of this slide — third-years should be using office hours to pressure-test arguments, not to catch up. Use around essay-planning time.

How marks work at Trinity, the reminder

You’ve had two years of this scale by now, but the reminder is cheap: 70 is a First, the top of the scale is deliberately hard to reach, and the number on its own tells you less than the feedback next to it.

Shorter than the first-year version — this is a reminder, not an explainer. Use once, after the mid-term essay marks come back.

3. Current-events beats

This week in authoritarianism

A reusable frame: what happened, which of this module’s concepts explains it best, and what a rival concept from a different week would say instead. Same three moves, new headline.

This is the template — duplicate and fill in a live case before each use, roughly every 2-3 sessions. The “rival concept” move is what makes this module-specific rather than generic current-events filler — use it to force students to choose between, say, personalism and institutionalism explanations of the same event.

Read past the headline

The headline tells you a leader did something authoritarian. It rarely tells you whether the institutions around them let it happen, resisted it, or don’t matter. That’s the question this module actually trains you to ask.

Works any time, but especially useful once the elite/mass or institutions material is a few weeks in and students have a framework to apply. Keep it short.
