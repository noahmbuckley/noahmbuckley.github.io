Doing political science research: advice, pitfalls, FAQs

Author

Noah Buckley

Published

September 3, 2026

This is the stuff I end up saying every single year, to every single group, usually in an email at 11pm. So I have written it down once. It is aimed at my final-year capstone students (8,000 words, year-long, authoritarianism theme) and at the MSc students whose dissertations I supervise in the spring. Most of it applies to both; where it does not, I say so.

Read the whole thing once, early — it is quicker than you think, and knowing that “selecting on the dependent variable” is a thing you can do wrong is worth more in October than a careful explanation of it in March. Then come back to individual sections when they become your problem.

If you read nothing else, read these five. Your question is too broad. You need variation in your outcome. You need a mechanism. Your X has to happen before your Y. Start collecting your data before Christmas.

1. What this project actually is

ImportantIt has to be political science, not a well-researched essay

The single most common thing I have to say in October. You will write me a proposal about, say, Belarusian state media after 2020 — the reading is real, the writing is good, the topic is genuinely interesting — and it is still not a research project. It is a report. A very good report.

The difference is that a research project makes a claim about cause and effect that could have come out otherwise, and then goes and tests it. A report tells you what happened. A historical narrative tells you what happened in order. A case study on its own tells you what happened somewhere in detail. All three are useful things in the world; none of them is what you are being marked on here.

The end goal is small and specific: find out something about the world that nobody knew before, in a rigorous way, and advance human knowledge by a tiny amount. Tiny is fine. Tiny is expected. What is not fine is that your project could, in principle, have been written without you doing any analysis at all.

Quick test: could a well-briefed journalist with three weeks and a library card have written your project? If yes, it is not there yet.

TipWhat the finished thing should look like

It should more or less look like a research paper you have read in one of your classes. Not a chapter of a book, not a policy brief, not a magazine feature: a journal article. Introduction, literature, theory, research design, results, conclusion. Numbered hypotheses if you have them. A table or a figure. A bibliography where the majority of entries are peer-reviewed political science.

This is genuinely helpful advice rather than a formality, because it means you have several hundred worked examples of the format sitting on Google Scholar. When you are stuck on how to structure section 4, go and look at how three published articles on something near your topic structured theirs. Imitate shamelessly. Nobody is grading you on inventing a new genre.

What I’ll ask you: name one published article your capstone is trying to look like. If you cannot, you have not read enough yet.

Further reading: Lipson, How to Write a BA Thesis; the Boston College senior thesis guide, especially the structure material near the end.

NoteOriginal does not mean revolutionary

People freeze up here. They think “new” means nobody has ever touched this, which is terrifying and also, if true, usually a bad sign — if literally nobody has studied your outcome, either it is unstudiable or it is uninteresting.

All science is iterative. It is borrowing and building off what has come before. Your contribution is allowed to be: someone tested this in Latin America and I am testing it in post-Soviet states; someone tested this with television and I am testing it with Telegram; someone established this in 1994 and the data are much better now; someone found an effect and I think their mechanism is wrong.

Every one of those is a legitimate, publishable-shaped contribution. What makes it yours is the specific combination of your outcome, your explanation, your cases, and your evidence. Your X is probably not new. Your Y is probably not new. Your X→Y in this context, measured this way, is.

WarningIf it’s obviously a great question, someone has already done it

This is not a reason to despair; it is a reason to read. When you find that your brilliant idea is already an article in Comparative Political Studies, you have three good options and one bad one.

Good: extend it (they did 1990–2010, the interesting variation is since 2014). Modify it (they used Freedom House, which is a blunt instrument for this — V-Dem’s component indices would let you separate two things they conflated). Test the mechanism they asserted but did not demonstrate. Or find the boundary condition: where does their claim stop being true, and why?

Bad: pretend you did not find it, and write your project as though the literature starts with you. I will find it. So will the second marker.

Honestly, discovering your question already exists is one of the more productive days of the project. You now have a blueprint, a set of variables that someone has already argued are measurable, and often a replication dataset you can download.

2. Finding an idea

TipHow to actually come up with something (go for a walk)

Block out a few hours. Not twenty minutes between other things — a few hours, with a notebook, and ideally a walk in the middle of them.

Concrete places to start:

  • Old essays you wrote, and the syllabi of modules you liked. What did you argue about in second year that you never got to finish?
  • Skim the “recommended reading” list of a module that interested you and see which titles jump out.
  • Pick a country or a topic you are mildly curious about and read its Wikipedia page end to end. Genuinely. Then follow one footnote.
  • The Annual Review of Political Science and the Oxford Research Encyclopedias. These exist to tell you what the state of a field is in twenty pages.
  • Connect political science to whatever else you are already interested in: climate, AI, migration, football, TikTok, inequality, Gaza, right-wing populism, Musk. How would any of those interact with authoritarian politics?

As you skim, keep asking three questions: does this still seem true? Is it true in every case I can think of, and if not, when not? And where did they stop, and why?

Conclusion sections very often contain a sentence beginning “future research should investigate…”. That sentence is a free research question written by someone with tenure.

WarningA topic is not a question

“Digital authoritarianism in Central Asia” is a topic. “Propaganda in Russia” is a topic. “The effect of election fraud on protest” is edging towards a question. “Does documented ballot-stuffing in a precinct increase subsequent protest turnout in that precinct?” is a question.

The test is grammatical and slightly stupid, but it works: a research question has a question mark, contains something that varies, and could be answered yes or no or “about this much”. A topic is a noun phrase.

Most people arrive in October with a topic and think they have a question. That is completely normal and it is exactly what the first month is for. But be honest with yourself about which one you have, because everything downstream — theory, design, data — is impossible to do to a noun phrase.

Quick test: write it out. Is there a verb, and does something in the sentence vary?

WarningYour question is narrower than you think. No — narrower than that

You have roughly six months, no research budget, no team, and 8,000 words. That is enough for one clean claim, tested reasonably well. It is not enough for “the consequences of authoritarian resilience in the twenty-first century”.

The move is not to abandon a big idea. It is to find the smallest testable piece of it, or one implication of it, or one nuance in it — and do that piece properly. The rest can wait for your MSc, or your PhD, or your life. Nobody has ever been marked down for a question that was too focused. Every year somebody is marked down for a question so broad that no evidence could possibly have settled it.

Think of the funnel: social science → political science → comparative politics → authoritarian politics → an area of inquiry within that → a topic → a research question. Most drafts I read stop two levels too early and call it a question.

What I’ll ask you: what is the one sentence, with a question mark, that your whole project answers?

Note“Does X affect Y?” beats “how” and “why”

“How did the Kremlin consolidate control over regional media?” and “why do autocrats hold elections?” feel like the deep questions, and they are. They are also extremely hard to answer with evidence in the space you have, because the answer is a story and stories are hard to falsify.

Ranked, roughly, from hardest to most tractable:

  1. How and why questions. Deep, satisfying, very hard to do well.
  2. What is the effect of X on Y? Good. Slightly descriptive, but you can answer it.
  3. Does X have an effect on Y? Great. Bounded, testable, and it forces you to name both variables on day one.

You can still write about how and why — that is what your theory section is for. But the thing you actually test should be the narrow one. Ask the “does” question in the design, answer the “why” question in the discussion.

Warning sign: three research questions on a slide, two of which start with “how”. I see this most years. Pick one, and make it the third kind.

TipExplain it to a parent (the plain-English test)

Imagine explaining your project to a parent, a housemate doing engineering, or a friend who has never taken a politics module. They should be able to follow: what you think causes what, and how you would know if you were wrong.

If you cannot do it without using the words “discursive”, “problematise”, or “hegemonic”, the problem is usually not their vocabulary. It is that the idea has not yet resolved into a claim about the world.

I ask for this explicitly on the worksheet: define your dependent variable in simple, not-too-jargony language, in fifteen words or fewer. People find this the hardest box on the sheet. That is informative.

Quick test: say it out loud to someone at dinner. If they ask “so… what’s the question?”, you are not done.

3. Theory: what do you think is going on?

ImportantTheory before test, always

Before you go and test anything, you need to know how you will interpret whatever you find. Otherwise you will run a regression, get a coefficient, and have no idea what it means — and, worse, you will be tempted to build a story around whatever number came out. That is not analysis, that is astrology with standard errors.

A theory is abstract and generalisable. It has roughly this shape: “Any time we observe X in the world, we should expect to see Y, because of A, B and C. People used to think it was because of D; I think that is wrong, and here is why.”

Note what is doing the work there: A, B and C. Your reasons should rest on something general — how people process information, how politicians respond to incentives, how organisations behave under uncertainty, basic economic logic. “In Kazakhstan the government did this because Nazarbayev wanted to” is not a theory. “Personalist leaders facing elite rather than mass threats invest in coup-proofing rather than propaganda” is.

What I’ll ask you: why do you expect to find what you expect to find — in terms that would apply to a country you have never studied?

WarningThe missing mechanism (my most-repeated comment)

If I had a stamp, it would say MECHANISM. I write it on nearly every draft.

Your independent variable does not reach out and touch your dependent variable by magic. Something happens in between. Foreign agent laws do not reduce independent media output directly; they impose financial and reputational costs, donors withdraw, outlets cannot pay staff, output falls. NAFTA does not reduce PRI vote share directly; maquiladora employment restructures local labour markets, which changes who depends on the party machine, which changes votes.

Naming the mechanism does three things for you. It makes your theory falsifiable in a second place. It usually suggests an extra piece of evidence you could go and get (if the mechanism is financial pressure, you can look at fundraising data). And it protects you from alternative explanations, because a rival account often predicts the same X→Y correlation through a different route.

You do not have to test the mechanism — often you cannot, in 8,000 words. You do have to state it explicitly and say what evidence would be consistent with it.

What I’ll ask you: what else has to happen for the effect of your X to reach all the way to your Y?

WarningAlternative explanations, and what you’d see if you were wrong

Every good project has a paragraph that begins, more or less, “of course, there is another way to read this.” Weak projects skip it and hope. Strong projects run at it.

Two questions to ask yourself repeatedly:

  • What else could produce the pattern I expect to find? If tertiary education expansion correlates with democratisation, is that education, or is it that richer countries do both? If protest follows fraud, is that the fraud, or is it that fraud happens where the opposition was already mobilised?
  • What would I expect to see if I were wrong? This is the more useful one, and almost nobody writes it down. If your theory is right, we should see A, B and C. If the main rival is right, we should see A but not B. That asymmetry is where your evidence gets its bite.

In quantitative work this shows up as control variables and as robustness checks. In qualitative work it shows up as rival hypotheses and disconfirming evidence. Same logic, different clothes.

Further reading: Van Evera, Guide to Methods for Students of Political Science (1997), on hypotheses, tests, and what makes a test strong.

NoteHypotheses: write them numbered, and don’t write eight

Write your expectations out as numbered hypotheses. H1: as X increases, Y decreases. H2: the effect in H1 is larger where Z. It looks bureaucratic. It is enormously clarifying, because a hypothesis that cannot be written in one sentence is usually two hypotheses wearing a coat, and a hypothesis you cannot imagine testing should not be in the project.

But there is a failure mode in the other direction, and I see it every year: seven hypotheses, of which three have no data behind them and two are the same claim restated. Every hypothesis costs you words and costs the reader attention. If H6 is supported by “not a lot of data”, ask yourself whether it is crucial to your story. If it is not, cut it and say so in a footnote.

Three or four hypotheses, well tested, beats eight gestured at.

Quick test: for each hypothesis, name in one breath the variable and the source that would test it. Any hypothesis where you cannot, gets cut.

NoteThe “so what?”

At some point — ideally in the first paragraph of your introduction — you have to tell the reader why anyone should care. There are two answers and you want both if you can get them.

The scholarly one: this literature believes X, and I am showing X is incomplete, or that it does not travel outside the cases it was built on. The worldly one: if I am right, then something we care about — repression, protest, war, whether elections constrain anyone — works differently than we thought.

You are researching this and not something else. Why? “Because it interests me” is a perfectly good reason to have chosen it and a completely inadequate reason to put in the paper.

Do not save the “so what” for the conclusion. By then everyone has already decided.

4. Research design: comparison, variation, units

ImportantScience is a comparative exercise

Your research question needs a comparison and variation built into it. This is the single structural requirement I will not budge on.

If you think some feature of a regime made it more repressive, more stable, richer, whatever — to show that, you have to compare it to something that turned out differently. There must be variation in your outcome: some units with Y₁, some with Y₂. And you will almost certainly need variation in your explanation too: some units with the policy, institution, shock or history, and some without.

Without variation you are writing historical narrative. It can be excellent narrative and it is still not what this is.

A different way to say the same thing: your question should make it obvious how the world would have been different if your cause had not been there. Then your job for the next six months is to show that you are right about that.

What I’ll ask you: what are your Y₁ cases and what are your Y₂ cases?

ImportantUnits. Say what they are

“Compared to what?” is only half the question. The other half is “compared across what?” Individuals to individuals? Countries to countries? Provinces, oblasts, counties, cities, precincts? Parties? Media outlets? Firms? Protest events? Country-years?

Your unit of observation determines your sample size, your data sources, what counts as a control, and whether your claim is even coherent. A theory about how individuals respond to propaganda cannot be tested on country-level democracy scores without a lot of explicit argument about why that is legitimate.

Write it in one line at the top of your design section: “The unit of observation is the Russian region-year, 2000–2023, n ≈ 1,900.” Everything after that gets easier.

Quick test: what is one row of your dataset? If you cannot describe one row, you do not yet have a design — even if your project is qualitative, where the “row” is a case.

WarningSelecting on the dependent variable

The classic. You are interested in why some autocracies collapse, so you assemble five autocracies that collapsed and look for what they had in common. You find something — you always find something — and you conclude it causes collapse.

You cannot know that. The thing they share may be equally common among the autocracies that survived. Without the survivors in your comparison, you have no idea whether your factor distinguishes anything at all.

This is not an obscure technicality; it is probably the most common fatal flaw in undergraduate comparative research, and it is seductive precisely because the cases that “worked” are the interesting ones with the good sources.

The fix is boring and effective: include cases where the outcome did not happen. Colour revolutions that succeeded and ones that fizzled. Outlets that closed and outlets that survived. Protests that spread and protests that did not.

Further reading: Barbara Geddes, “How the Cases You Choose Affect the Answers You Get: Selection Bias in Comparative Politics,” Political Analysis 2 (1990).

WarningX has to happen before Y

Time order is not a nicety. If your independent variable is measured after your dependent variable moved, you have not shown causation; you may have shown the reverse.

I say this most often about slow-moving national indicators. If you want to argue that tertiary education expansion drives later democratisation, your education data need to come from well before the democracy scores you are explaining — not from an overlapping window, and certainly not from a cross-section averaged over the same years.

Related and just as common: endogeneity you can see coming. Independent media outlets that were already weak get designated as foreign agents; regions that were already restive get more police. If your X is plausibly a response to your Y, say so explicitly, and do something about it — lagging, a pre-period baseline, a shock with timing you did not choose, or at minimum an honest paragraph about what you cannot rule out.

Quick test: put your X and your Y on a timeline. Which is on the left?

WarningYour DV must not contain your IV

Subtler than it sounds, and I catch it every year. A student wants to show that the Dobbs decision increased authoritarian tendencies in US states — and then measures “authoritarian tendencies” with an index that includes abortion restrictions. You have now proved that abortion restrictions cause abortion restrictions.

The same trap: using a composite democracy score as your outcome when your explanation is one of the score’s own components. V-Dem’s electoral democracy index is built from sub-indices; if your X is media freedom, do not use an index that has media freedom inside it. Use a component that excludes it, and say why.

Conceptual overlap counts too, not just literal double-counting. If your outcome is “state capacity” and your explanation is “tax collection”, you should be nervous.

Quick test: open the codebook. Literally read what goes into your outcome variable. Is your cause in there?

NoteControl variables: what they’re for, and how not to over-model

Controls exist to close off alternative explanations, one specific rival at a time. That is the whole job. So each control should have a sentence attached: “I include GDP per capita because richer countries both educate more and democratise more, so the raw correlation may be spurious.”

Two failure modes. First, the kitchen sink — twenty covariates because they were in the dataset, several of which sit on the causal path between your X and your Y and therefore delete the effect you are trying to find. Second, none at all.

Fixed effects deserve a special mention because they are cheap and powerful. Country fixed effects absorb everything time-invariant about each country; year fixed effects absorb whatever happened to everyone that year. In panel data they often do the work of a dozen controls at once. They also eat slow-moving variables, which is why V-Dem scores plus country fixed effects can leave you with nothing — worth knowing before you build the whole project on it.

And please: no MANOVA. You do not need it.

NoteEmpirical scope: say where your claim stops

State the boundaries of your evidence explicitly: which cases, which years, which population. Not because markers like caveats, but because a claim with stated scope is a scientific claim and a claim without one is a vibe.

“Electoral autocracies in the post-Soviet space, 1995–2020” is a scope. It tells the reader that you are not claiming this about Singapore, and it makes your project more credible, not less, because you are no longer over-claiming.

Scope also protects you in the conclusion, where you are allowed — encouraged — to speculate about whether the argument travels. That speculation only reads as interesting if you were disciplined about scope earlier.

5. Measurement

ImportantHow will you know a unit has been treated?

Measurement is where good ideas go to die, and it is worth confronting in November rather than February.

For your independent variable: in basic, conceptual terms, how will you know whether each unit has been treated? What does a treated unit look like, concretely, and what distinguishes it from an untreated one? If your X is “exposure to state propaganda”, is that broadcast reach? Self-reported viewing? Living in a region where the transmitter works?

For your dependent variable: how will you collect information showing different outcomes across multiple units? Not “I will assess democratic backsliding” — which source, which variable, which years, at what level of aggregation?

Every year at least one project stalls here, and it always stalls in the same way: the concept is clear and the operationalisation is a placeholder that never gets filled in.

What I’ll ask you: name the variable. Dataset, variable name, coverage.

NoteChoosing among indices when they disagree

V-Dem, Polity, and Freedom House often disagree about the same country in the same year, sometimes dramatically. That is not a scandal; they are measuring different things with different procedures. But it does mean “I used a democracy score” is not a measurement decision. Which one, and why, is.

V-Dem’s virtue is that it is disaggregated: dozens of component indices, so you can pick the one that matches your actual concept instead of a blur. Its vice for undergraduate projects is that many components move slowly, so with fixed effects and a short panel you can end up with almost no variation to explain.

Freedom House is coarse and its aggregation is opaque, but it is long-running and familiar. Polity has well-known coding issues around interregna and does not cover everything.

Whatever you pick, do two things: say in one sentence why this measure matches your concept, and check whether your result survives swapping it for another. If it does not, that is a finding too — an honest one.

TipIf you’re making your own data, sell it — and document it

Hand-coding a few hundred news articles, party manifestos, court decisions or protest events is one of the most impressive things a capstone can contain, and students consistently undersell it. If you built a dataset, that is a contribution in its own right. Say so, in the introduction, in a sentence that begins “I construct a novel dataset of…”.

Then earn it. Explain your coding scheme in enough detail that someone could reproduce it: what the population of documents was, where you got them, what the categories are, what a borderline case looks like and how you resolved it. Show a table of how the coding came out. If you can, code a subsample twice and report agreement — even informally, even just “I recoded 50 items a month later and agreed with myself on 46”.

Quick test: could a classmate take your codebook and produce roughly your dataset?

6. Methods: quantitative, qualitative, and being honest about which

ImportantYou almost certainly have to pick one

The categories are fuzzy and there is a real spectrum, but in practice you should choose, and choose based on two things: what your research question actually needs, and what you can realistically do well in six months.

Both can be convincing. Both can be a lot of work. Both can be done badly. I will nudge you towards quantitative work in my group, and you will not be penalised one point for going qualitative — but I want the choice to be a choice, made in November, and not something you drift into in February because the data did not arrive.

What I do not want is a project that is quantitative in the results section and qualitative in the theory section, with neither doing enough work.

TipQuantitative work is more doable than you think. Just do OLS

Do not be afraid of this. You do not need a multilevel elastic-net Bayesian ordered multivariate probit with jackknifed heteroskedastic standard errors. You need OLS. Possibly with fixed effects. That is genuinely it, at this level, and a clean, well-motivated OLS with a good research design beats a fancy estimator applied to a muddled one every single time.

The practical advantages are real:

  • It is transparent and reproducible. Your marker can see what you did.
  • It is iterative. New variable, new measure, new specification — an afternoon, not a month. Qualitative work rarely offers that.
  • It is easy to get help with. Every error message you will hit has been answered on Stack Overflow. R and Google and, yes, an LLM will get you a long way on code.
  • There is an enormous amount of data sitting there, free, ready to download.

I ran an R refresher session most years and will again — [TBD: confirm date]. Come to it even if you think you remember everything from third year. You do not.

Quick test: if you cannot say what one row of your dataset is, you are not ready to run anything.

WarningProcess tracing is harder than you think — at least to do well

Process tracing has a reputation among students as the qualitative option that lets you write a detailed narrative and call it a method. It is close to the opposite of that. Done properly it is one of the more demanding things in the discipline.

Real process tracing means specifying, in advance, the observable implications your theory predicts at each link in the causal chain, and then asking what each piece of evidence does to your confidence — including evidence that would have shown up under a rival explanation and therefore tells you nothing. Collier’s framework of straw-in-the-wind, hoop, smoking-gun and doubly-decisive tests is about exactly this: some evidence is necessary for a claim, some is sufficient, most is neither, and you have to say which is which before you go looking.

It also demands sources most undergraduates cannot get: archives, elite interviews, leaked documents, memoirs in a language you read. On authoritarian cases in particular, the interesting evidence is often the evidence the regime has suppressed.

I am not telling you not to do it. I am telling you that “I will process trace the Belarusian case” is a claim I will push on hard, and that a well-executed comparison of thirty cases is usually a safer route to a good mark than a poorly-executed process trace of one.

Further reading: David Collier, “Understanding Process Tracing,” PS: Political Science & Politics 44(4), 2011.

WarningA single case study is not automatically a design

Single-case work can be excellent, but it has to be doing analytical work, not descriptive work. A single case can plausibly: test a theory in a setting where it should be most likely to hold (and fail); disconfirm a claim of necessity; trace a mechanism in detail that large-N work can only assume; or generate a hypothesis for others to test.

What it cannot do is establish that X causes Y across the world by describing one place carefully. And it cannot do any of the above if you have not said, explicitly, which of those jobs it is doing and why this case rather than another.

If you go single-case, your case selection paragraph is the most important paragraph in the project. “I chose Hungary because I find it interesting” is not that paragraph. “Hungary is a most-likely case for the EU-conditionality argument — high dependence on EU funds, strong formal compliance mechanisms — so its failure there is informative” is.

Also very often available: a within-case comparison. Regions, years, sectors, before and after a shock. You get variation without leaving your case.

Further reading: Gerring, Social Science Methodology: A Unified Framework (2012); King, Keohane & Verba, Designing Social Inquiry (1994), especially on inference from small numbers of observations.

WarningMixed methods: usually too ambitious

Roughly every year somebody proposes a large-N analysis plus two in-depth qualitative case studies plus a content analysis. It sounds thorough. In 8,000 words it produces three thin things instead of one solid one, and the thin things tend not to talk to each other.

If you genuinely want a mixed design, the version that works is asymmetric: one method carries the argument and the other does one small, clearly-defined job. A quantitative result plus two paragraphs illustrating the mechanism in one case. A qualitative argument plus one descriptive figure establishing that the pattern is general.

“I will triangulate” is not a plan. Name which method answers the research question, and give the other one a job title.

7. Data: where to get it

TipThere is a huge amount of data out there, free, right now

This is the part students most consistently underestimate. You can often download, ready to use, exactly the thing you need. Below is where I would look first. Most are free; a few want an institutional login, which Trinity has.

Regimes, democracy, institutions

  • V-Dem (Varieties of Democracy) — the workhorse. Hundreds of indicators, most countries, back to 1789 in places. There is an R package (vdemdata).
  • Polity5 — long-running regime scores; know its coding quirks.
  • Freedom House — Freedom in the World, plus Nations in Transit and Freedom on the Net (the latter is very useful for digital-authoritarianism projects).
  • Quality of Government (QoG) — Gothenburg’s mega-merge of hundreds of other datasets into one country-year file. Frequently the fastest way to get a control variable. Start here more often than you’d think.
  • Database of Political Institutions (DPI) — executive/legislative institutions, party systems, checks and balances.
  • Comparative Constitutions Project — what constitutions actually contain, coded.
  • ParlGov — parties, elections and cabinets for parliamentary democracies.
  • Manifesto Project (CMP/MARPOR) — party programmes coded by policy content.
  • Chapel Hill Expert Survey (CHES) — expert placements of parties on policy dimensions.

Public opinion

  • World Values Survey and the European Values Study
  • European Social Survey (ESS) — very high quality, free, well documented.
  • Afrobarometer, Arab Barometer, Latinobarómetro, AsianBarometer
  • Eurobarometer (via GESIS)
  • CSES (Comparative Study of Electoral Systems) — survey modules matched to election contexts.
  • ANES (US) and the British Election Study
  • Levada Center — the essential source for Russian public opinion, and much of it is posted publicly. [Noah has thoughts about how to use it carefully — see below.]

Conflict, protest, repression

  • ACLED — geocoded political violence and protest events, updated weekly.
  • UCDP/PRIO — armed conflict, the standard for war data.
  • Mass Mobilization Project — protest events against governments, 1990–.
  • GDELT — vast automated event data from news. Powerful and messy; treat its coding as noisy and validate a sample by hand before you build on it.

Economics, development, everything else

  • World Bank World Development Indicators (WDI) — R package WDI pulls it directly.
  • Our World in Data — clean, well-sourced, downloadable, good for figures.
  • IMF, OECD, UNESCO statistics portals.

Archives that hold everything else

  • Harvard Dataverse and ICPSR — where replication data and thousands of standalone datasets live. Search these before concluding your data do not exist.

TipAsk the authors for their replication data

Underused and slightly magical. Most political science journals now require authors to post replication files, usually on Dataverse. That means for a large fraction of the articles you are reading, you can download the exact dataset behind the exact table, open it, and see how a real project was assembled.

Two things this gives you. First, a shortcut: someone has already spent a year merging V-Dem with election returns for your region, and you can extend it rather than rebuild it. Second, an education: reading a real replication file teaches you more about how quantitative research is actually done than any methods lecture.

If the data are not posted, email the authors. A short, polite, specific email — who you are, what you are working on, exactly which data you are asking about, what you would use them for. Academics receive very few emails from undergraduates who have clearly read the paper, and a lot of us answer them. Some will say no or not reply; that costs you nothing but the three minutes.

Always cite the original data source, and check its terms — some datasets require a specific citation form or forbid redistribution.

WarningData availability is a design constraint, not an afterthought

Check that your data exist before you commit to the question. Not after the proposal. Not in February.

The specific failure I see: a beautiful design that requires a variable measured annually at subnational level for twenty countries, which exists for four of them, for nine years, with a definitional break in the middle. By the time this surfaces it is March and there is no time to change course.

So, in November, do this concretely: go and download the actual file. Open it. Count the rows. Look at how many are missing on your key variable. Check whether the coverage years overlap with the period your theory is about.

Ten minutes of this in November saves a catastrophe in March. And if the data are not there, you have found out early enough to adjust your question — which is a normal, respectable part of research, not a failure.

What I’ll ask you: have you opened the file yet?

NoteCollecting your own data (and combining existing sets)

Original data is cool but not remotely necessary. If you do want to:

  • You can survey your friends, classmates and family. It is not a representative sample and you must say so, but for some questions — an experimental vignette, say — a convenience sample is genuinely informative.
  • You can go out and collect things: observe, count, record, code.
  • You can scrape: news archives, government sites, court records, party websites, Telegram channels, speech transcripts. Check the site’s terms, and check with me before you build anything large.
  • You can request data from a government body or a researcher.

Combining existing datasets in new ways is a real contribution and often the sweet spot for a capstone: nobody has merged X with Y at this level before, and the merge itself is the novelty. But be warned that merging is where projects lose weeks — country names, coding schemes and year coverage never match, and every merge quietly drops rows. Always check your row count before and after, and know why it changed.

If you are collecting data from human beings, you need ethics approval. See §9.

8. Reading and the literature

ImportantDon’t reinvent the wheel

Nobody will have done exactly your project — but plenty of people have done something adjacent, and you can learn an enormous amount from them. Maybe someone has studied this in democracies. Or in Latin America. Or with television instead of social media. Or thirty years ago, when the data were worse.

Find those people. Read how they set up the problem, what they used as a measure, what they controlled for, how they structured the paper, what they admitted they could not show. Use them as a blueprint. This is not cheating; this is what a literature is for.

The students who struggle most in February are almost always the ones who did not do this in November, and who are now trying to invent a measurement strategy from first principles under time pressure, badly, when a good one was sitting in an article they never found.

What I’ll ask you: whose design are you borrowing?

TipSearch your X and your Y separately

Concrete plan of attack, and the single most useful search tip I give.

Do one pass on Google Scholar for work on your dependent variable: what do scholars think causes your Y? Who are the main camps? What are the standard measures and controls? Then do a separate pass on your independent variable: what effects does your X have on other things? What is its measurement literature?

Your combination of X and Y is what is new. Your X and your Y individually are almost certainly not — so go and get to know both literatures on their own terms. This also straightforwardly gives you the structure of your literature review, which otherwise tends to come out as an undifferentiated pile.

Also worth using: the Annual Review of Political Science, the Oxford Research Encyclopedias, literature review sections of recent articles (someone has already done the work), Scholar’s “cited by” to walk forward in time from a classic, and the subject librarians, who are extremely good and almost entirely unused by undergraduates.

TipRead smart

You cannot read everything and you should not try. The skill is knowing what depth a given item deserves.

Most items: abstract, introduction, and the first page of the conclusion. That is enough to know what they claim and whether you need more. A good number: also skim the research design section, because that is where the transferable craft is. A small number — the five or ten papers your project is genuinely in conversation with — get a proper close reading, tables and all.

Early on, especially, breadth beats depth. Get as many abstracts, introductions, literature reviews and conclusions into your head as you can. You are building a map, not memorising a paper.

And keep the reading going. It is not a phase that ends in November. You will understand your own project differently in February, and things you skimmed in October will suddenly matter.

WarningBe guided by prominent work in known outlets

Work can be excellent even if it is not much cited or appears in a journal nobody has heard of. But if the main paper you are hanging your capstone on is in the Polish Journal of Political Municipal Institutional Studies, you are probably partly on the wrong track — and, more importantly, you are likely missing the prominent work that everyone in this area has actually read.

Rough heuristics: citation counts (relative to age), whether the journal appears on reading lists you have seen, whether the authors’ other work turns up repeatedly. None is decisive. All are better than nothing.

Two specific warnings. Predatory journals are real and will happily publish anything; if you have never heard of the journal and neither has Scholar, be careful. And a working paper or preprint is fine to cite, but say that is what it is, and check whether it was later published in a different form.

Quick test: for your core literature, would somebody who works in this area recognise most of the names?

WarningThe annotated bibliography that is just a list

The commonest form of a weak literature review: ten paragraphs, each summarising one article, in no particular order, connected by nothing. “Smith (2011) argues… Jones (2015) finds… Chen (2019) shows…”. It reads as ten book reports stapled together, which is what it is.

A literature review is an argument about a literature. It should be organised by ideas, not by authors: here is what this field believes and on what basis; here are the two camps and what they disagree about; here is the thing everyone has assumed and nobody has tested; that gap is where I am going.

Even in the short annotated bibliography I ask for in November, push towards this. For each item, do not just say what it argues — say how it relates to your project, whether your explanation would extend it or contradict it, and what its limitation is that you might build on.

Quick test: could you reorder your paragraphs at random without the reader noticing? Then it is a list.

9. Writing it up

TipThe structure, with word budgets

Your project should look like a journal article. For 8,000 words (the capstone hard limit), a reasonable budget:

Section Words What it does
Introduction 800–1,000 Puzzle, question, argument, findings, why it matters
Literature review 1,200–1,500 An argument about a literature; ends in the gap
Theory & hypotheses 1,200–1,500 Mechanism, numbered expectations
Research design & data 1,200–1,500 Units, cases, measures, method, limitations
Results 1,500–2,000 The evidence, plus robustness/alternatives
Conclusion 600–800 What we learned, scope, what’s next

These are guides, not rules. A data-heavy project can move words from literature into results; a theory-driven one the reverse. But if any section is wildly outside these bands, something is wrong — a 3,000-word literature review in an 8,000-word paper usually means the analysis is thin and you are padding.

For the MSc dissertation at ~12,000–15,000 words, scale up unevenly: the literature review and theory grow the most (a fuller, more systematic engagement is expected), the results section grows, and the introduction barely changes.

House style for the capstone: title page, abstract, table of contents, bibliography (alphabetical by surname), and an explicit final word count. The word count includes text and footnotes; it excludes title page, abstract, acknowledgements, contents, tables, appendices and bibliography. 8,000 is a hard limit — over it costs 10 marks, no exceptions. Aim for 7,500–8,000; a much shorter project usually is one.

TipWrite the introduction last

Write a placeholder introduction early, because you need something to aim at. Then rewrite it from scratch at the end, because you cannot introduce an argument you had not yet made.

The final introduction does five things, in about a page: sets up the puzzle; states the research question; states your answer, plainly (“I argue that…”); says what you find; and says why it matters. Yes, you give away the findings. This is not a detective novel — the reader wants to know where they are going.

The most common weak introduction opens with a sentence about how “in recent decades, scholars have increasingly turned their attention to…”. Nobody has ever been gripped by that sentence. Open with the puzzle: a fact that is surprising, a pattern that should not exist, two cases that should have gone the same way and did not.

Quick test: does your first paragraph contain a claim someone could disagree with?

NoteTables, figures, and not burying the finding

Figures do not count against your word limit, which is a substantial gift — use it.

A well-made descriptive figure early in the results section, showing the basic variation you are trying to explain, does more for a reader than three paragraphs of prose. Show your outcome over time, or across your cases, before you show any model.

Regression tables: keep them readable. One table with your main specifications, not six. Label your variables in English, not lngdppc_wdi. Say in the text what the coefficient means in units a person can picture — “a one standard-deviation increase in X is associated with a drop of about 0.15 points on the V-Dem index, roughly the gap between Hungary in 2010 and Hungary in 2018” — rather than only whether stars appeared.

Every table and figure needs a caption that could be understood on its own, and needs to be referred to in the text. stargazer, modelsummary and gt in R all produce respectable output.

WarningPresentation slides: few words, one idea per slide

For workshop presentations: keep the slides very brief and very simple. Slides that are just walls of text are impossible for an audience to read and digest, which means you get worse feedback, which is the entire reason you are up there.

I write “slides are just all the text” in my notes most years, so consider this a standing warning. One idea per slide. A question, a diagram, a table, a photo of your data. Not paragraphs.

Show things: a bit of the raw data, your survey instrument, a map of your cases, a scatterplot of your outcome, a draft of your coding scheme. Concrete artefacts produce concrete feedback. An abstract summary produces abstract feedback, which is useless to you.

And talk about your problems, not just your plan. The presentation slot is worth much more to you if you use it on the thing that is stuck.

NoteCitation, style, and not being sloppy

Pick one citation style and be consistent — APA or Chicago author-date are both fine and both standard in the discipline. [TBD: check whether the capstone documentation specifies one.] Use a reference manager (Zotero is free, and Trinity will not stop you); the twenty minutes to set it up will save you a miserable evening in March.

Cite everything you take from anywhere, including datasets, including working papers, including the codebook that told you what a variable means. Where a claim about the world is contestable, it needs a source; where it is your own analysis, that should be unambiguous.

Proofread on paper. Read the introduction and conclusion out loud. Check that the numbers in your text match the numbers in your tables, because after the fourth revision they often do not, and a marker who catches one mismatch starts checking all of them.

10. Process: time, supervision, ethics, AI

ImportantThe timeline, and the thing that goes wrong

Roughly how the year should go. [TBD: exact dates each year — this is the shape, not the calendar.]

  • September–October. Read broadly. Three candidate research questions. Pick one.
  • November. Annotated bibliography. Fill in the research design worksheet completely. Identify your data and download them. Ethics application if you need one.
  • December. Research proposal. Over the Christmas break: assemble the dataset, or do the bulk of your source collection.
  • January. Analysis under way. First results, however ugly.
  • February. Presentation with preliminary results. Drafting. This is when you find out what is broken, and there is still time to fix it.
  • March. Writing, revising, submission.

The failure mode is always the same and it is always the same month: everything slips right, data collection lands in February, analysis in the last fortnight, and the project is written in a week. The deadline does not move. Have your data in hand by early February at the very latest — meaning downloaded, merged, cleaned, and looked at, not “identified”.

And the blunt version: if you leave it to the last minute and then need help from me, I may simply not be able to give it. In late March I have several of you at once and a finite number of hours.

TipUse Christmas properly (it is longer than you think)

The break is the single largest uninterrupted block of time you will have all year, and it lands exactly when you need to do the boring, unglamorous, essential work: downloading files, merging them, cleaning them, discovering that the country names do not match, fixing that.

You do not need me for any of that, which is convenient, because I am not around.

A realistic Christmas target: a dataset that loads, with your outcome and your main explanatory variable in it, and one plot of your outcome. That is it. If you come back in January with that, you are in good shape and the rest of the year is a different, much calmer experience. If you come back having read some more things, you are behind and you will feel it in February.

TipHow to ask your supervisor for help well

Send me the specific thing.

“I’m stuck on my methodology” gives me nothing to work with and gets you a vague reply that helps neither of us. “Here is my dataset and my R script; line 40 throws this error and I’ve tried X and Y” gets you a fix in ten minutes. “I’m choosing between V-Dem’s electoral democracy index and its liberal component as my DV; here’s why I lean towards the first; what am I missing?” gets you a real answer.

The pattern that works: what you are trying to do, what you have tried, what happened, and the actual file. Attach the thing. Paste the error. Show me the table.

Also: come to clinics. They are drop-in office-hours sessions specifically for the detailed, concrete stuff — your code, your data, your survey, your coding scheme — while group meetings handle the big picture. Every year some of you never come to a single one, and every year those are the projects that surface a fixable problem two weeks before the deadline.

Give me reasonable notice, and do not expect same-day answers in the last fortnight.

TipGet the most out of group workshops and being a discussant

Group supervision only works if the group works. Two practical points.

When you present: do not use the slot to demonstrate that you are on top of things. Use it on what is broken. Bring the question you cannot answer, the result that came out backwards, the variable you cannot find. Circulate a draft in advance — the more you send, the better the feedback you get, and it does not have to be polished.

When you are the discussant: read the draft properly and write your comments down in advance. Five minutes of organised comment. Say what you did not understand — that is the most valuable feedback there is, and it is the one thing the author cannot generate themselves. Suggest, do not just critique: a different measure, a case they should look at, a way of framing the question. It feels awkward to comment on a project you barely know. It is also exactly what academics do to each other constantly, it is good practice, and you will learn things you can use in your own work.

Every year the students who engage seriously with other people’s projects write better ones themselves. I do not think that is a coincidence.

ImportantEthics approval: only some of you, but apply early

If you are collecting original data from human beings — a survey, interviews, focus groups, an experiment, anything where you are interacting with participants — you need ethics approval before you collect a single response. Not after. Approval is not retrospective, and data collected without it cannot be used.

Most capstone projects do not need it, because most use existing datasets, documents, or published sources. Analysing an existing public survey does not require approval. Running your own does.

If there is any chance you need this, tell me as early as possible and certainly by early December, so we can get the application in and approved before you would want to start collecting in January. The process takes time and can come back with requested changes.

NoteUsing AI on this project

These tools exist, you use them, and you will use them in whatever you do after this degree. Pretending otherwise would waste both our time. But this project is not assessing whether you can obtain a competent paragraph about political science — that is now free, and worth roughly what it costs. It is assessing whether you can find a question, build an argument, get evidence, and put your own case in your own words. That capacity is built by doing the work, and it does not survive being outsourced.

So the line is: use these tools to help you think and to help you fix your prose; do not use them to do the thinking or to produce the prose.

In practice, for this kind of project:

  • Code. Genuinely useful and I encourage it. Googling and an LLM will get you a long way in getting R to work. You still have to understand what the code does — if you cannot explain your own analysis, that is a problem regardless of who wrote it.
  • Brainstorming and objections. Fine. Argue with it about your theory; ask it to attack your design.
  • Finding literature. Careful. These tools invent citations that do not exist, confidently. Every reference goes in your bibliography only after you have found the actual paper. I will notice a fabricated citation, and it is a serious matter.
  • Writing your prose. No. The argument and the words are what is being assessed.

Disclose what you did on the School’s Assignment Submission Form, in its own vocabulary. I am not going to run your work through a detector and accuse you on that basis — the detectors do not work well enough for that. I am interested in you being straight with me, and in you being able to think when there is no model in the room.

11. MSc dissertation: what’s different

NoteLonger, more independent, and the literature review has to do more

Everything above applies. What changes:

Length. Typically 12,000–15,000 words rather than 8,000 [TBD: confirm the exact limit for your programme — check your handbook, do not take my number for it]. That is not just “more of the same”. It buys you a genuinely fuller literature review, a more developed theory section, and room for extensions and robustness work that a capstone has to leave out.

The literature review is held to a higher standard. At MSc level I expect a systematic engagement with the field, organised by argument, that positions your work precisely — not a survey of the ten things you found. This is the section where the difference between the two levels shows up most clearly.

More independence. Fewer scheduled contact points, no weekly workshop group, often no cohort of people doing adjacent projects. Which means the discipline of timetabling yourself is on you, and the cost of drifting for a month is higher. Build your own structure: set your own interim deadlines and tell me what they are.

Compressed timeline. The dissertation typically runs over the spring and summer rather than the full year, so the “have your data by February” rule becomes something like “have your data a clear two months before submission”. The same rule, less slack.

Methods expectations are a bit higher, in the sense that you are expected to justify your design against alternatives rather than simply state it. Not that you need fancier statistics — OLS is still fine.

Further reading

Things worth actually looking at, roughly in order of how useful they are to you now.

  • Middlebury College, Writing Tips for Senior Theses in Political Science — short, practical, on components of a good thesis project.
  • Scott Minkoff, “A Guide to Developing and Writing Research Papers in Political Science” — the most directly applicable of these.
  • Boston College, How to Write a Senior Thesis — read pp. 10–68, especially 63–68 on structure.
  • Charles Lipson, How to Write a BA Thesis (2nd ed.) — the best book-length treatment of the process, including the parts about managing yourself.
  • Alan Bryman, Social Research Methods — accessible, comprehensive, good on the range of methods.
  • John Gerring, Social Science Methodology: A Unified Framework (2012) — more advanced; excellent on causality and building theoretical arguments.
  • King, Keohane & Verba, Designing Social Inquiry (1994) — the standard reference on inference in qualitative and quantitative work alike. Contested, and worth knowing why.
  • Barbara Geddes, “How the Cases You Choose Affect the Answers You Get,” Political Analysis 2 (1990) — on selection bias. Twenty pages, and it will change how you pick cases.
  • David Collier, “Understanding Process Tracing,” PS: Political Science & Politics 44(4) (2011) — read this before you tell me you are going to process trace.
  • Stephen Van Evera, Guide to Methods for Students of Political Science (1997) — short, blunt, good on hypotheses and tests.
  • Kellstedt & Whitten, The Fundamentals of Political Science Research — the clearest introduction to the quantitative side, and a sane refresher if third-year methods has faded.
