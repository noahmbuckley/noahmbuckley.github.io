Module description

This module introduces you to the big questions in politics and to the conceptual, theoretical, and analytical tools that political scientists use to answer them. It is designed both for students who intend to study politics for the rest of their degree and for those taking a single, standalone introduction to the discipline.

We start from the basics — what politics is, what power is, why states exist and why some of them work and others don’t. From there we take on ideologies and political identity, democracy and dictatorship and the grey zone in between, corruption and state capture, political institutions, and the question of who gets what in the economy. We close on the two things that make political science a science: collective action problems, and how you would ever know whether a claim about politics is true.

The goal is not to memorise facts. It is to learn to think like a political scientist: to ask sharper questions, compare across cases, and carry a small toolkit of portable concepts into political situations you have never seen before.

Aims

The module has two aims: (1) to give you a basic knowledge of the key problems and questions around which politics revolves; and (2) to equip you with the fundamental conceptual, theoretical, and analytical tools used in the consideration of political questions.

Learning outcomes

On successful completion of this module, you should be able to:

  • Identify and understand the key problems and questions around which politics revolves.
  • Employ the fundamental conceptual, theoretical, and analytical tools used in the consideration of political questions.
  • Critically engage with and analyse contemporary national and global political events and issues using the tools and frameworks of political science.

How the module works

Lectures

There are twenty lectures — two a week, Tuesdays and Thursdays at 11:00 — across ten teaching weeks. Attendance is not taken and there is no penalty for missing a lecture, but the lectures are where the module actually happens. Material will be covered in lecture that is not in the readings, and the slides are a prompt for what I say, not a transcript of it. You will not be able to reconstruct a lecture from the slides, and tutorials are not a place to catch up on lectures you missed. To do well here you need to do the readings, come to the lectures, and take part in tutorial discussion.

Slides will be posted on the course website.

Tutorials

There are four fortnightly tutorial sessions across the term, run in small groups by our teaching assistants. Tutorials are not repeat lectures: they are where you ask questions, argue with the readings, give your views or thoughts to the room, and hear what your classmates make of them.

Attendance at tutorials is compulsory and is linked to assessment (see below).

You do not sign up for a tutorial group. The department allocates groups and they appear in your my.tcd.ie timetable.

Tutorials are a place for open, warm, and sometimes contentious discussion. People in the room have different backgrounds, experiences, and personal stakes in these questions. Everyone is expected to engage seriously with the arguments and generously with the people making them.

Readings and the textbook

The core text for this module is:

Andrew Heywood & Matthew Laing, Politics, 6th edition (Bloomsbury, 2025).

Chapter numbering is unchanged from the 2019 5th edition, so an older or second-hand copy is entirely fine. A PDF copy is provided in the Textbook item on Blackboard.

Each week has a small number of required readings, generally from this textbook, which you should complete before the relevant lecture, plus recommended readings for going deeper, for the assignments, and for your own interest. Journal articles beyond the textbook you find yourself, through the Library or Google Scholar; book chapters and anything else hard to find are provided via Blackboard, and links will be available on the course webpage. The reading list in the schedule below is indicative and may be adjusted during term; Blackboard and the course website carry the current version.

Assessment

Component Weight
Discussion-point submissions + tutorial attendance 10%
Short written assignment (blog post) 30%
End-of-term final essay 60%

Full assignment instructions, prompts, and essay questions will be released in lecture and on Blackboard.

Discussion points and tutorial attendance (10%)

All four of this term’s tutorials are required.

For each of the term’s tutorials you must submit a set of discussion points — three numbered points, 200–400 words or so, before the tutorial — and attend the tutorial in question. Both halves are required: points without attendance, or attendance without points, earn no credit for that tutorial.

You are not being asked for polished analysis. You are being asked to raise something worth group discussion. You can mention things you didn’t understand in the readings, object to a point that has been made, add something to the content, or many other things.

Submissions are pass/fail: two points for each tutorial attended with an accepted submission, to a maximum of eight. Your TA can reject a submission that is too short or makes no genuine attempt at the reading. Deadline is 20:00 the evening before your tutorial meets, via Blackboard; nothing is accepted after the tutorial has met.

Department policy: this component is capped at 80. Perfect attendance and participation earns a mark of 80 for this component, with reductions proportional to that. This reflects the realities of the Trinity marking scale.

Full mechanics — what to write, what does not count, excused absence, the GenAI line — are in the discussion points sheet.

Short assignment — blog post (30%)

An 800-word blog post taking one concept from the module and using it to make sense of one real political situation.

  • Length: 800 words.
  • Style: rigorous but less formal than an academic essay. Headings, short paragraphs, bold text, links and images are all welcome; a real argument is required. It is not an opinion piece with citations bolted on, and it is not an essay with subheadings.
  • Sources: cite at least two academic readings or materials from the module.
  • Prompts and details will be provided at least three weeks before the due date, in the short assignment sheet.
  • Due: Friday 23 October 2026, 23:59.

Final essay (60%)

A 1,500-word essay answering one of several set questions, drawing on the concepts, tools, and cases from across the module. I am looking for more than recall: take a position, compare across cases, and reason with the module’s toolkit.

  • Length: 1,500 words.
  • Prompts and details will be provided at least three weeks before the due date, in the final essay sheet.
  • Due: Tuesday 15 December 2026, 23:59.

There is no examination. Questions, how to structure an argumentative essay at this level, citation expectations and the GenAI permissions are in the final essay sheet.

Written work: format requirements

In line with department guidelines, written work for this module must:

  • state the final word count, and observe the word limit — limits carry a 10% tolerance either way, and going outside that range costs up to 5 marks;
  • be submitted to Blackboard in Word or PDF, with the Assignment Submission Form as page 1;
  • use consistent citation and referencing (the TCD Library style guide will help);
  • include an alphabetised bibliography;
  • be spellchecked.

Back your work up, and submit early enough to survive a technical problem. Keep your submission receipt. Computer crashes, corrupted files, and internet problems are not grounds for late submission.

Policies

Excused absence from tutorials

If you need an absence from a tutorial excused for certified medical reasons, your College Tutor must contact both your TA and me to make the request. Only requests from College Tutors are considered. If you want module credit for that tutorial, the discussion points must still be submitted — at or before the next tutorial, or within one week of the end of term if it relates to the last tutorial.

Late work

All late work, unless excused in advance by me, is penalised at 5 marks per day or part thereof, up to a maximum of 20 marks. There are many good reasons a student cannot submit on time and I am happy to make reasonable accommodations. What is not reasonable is getting in touch after the deadline, or a few hours before it. Do not leave requests until the last minute. Department policy does not allow extensions to be granted retrospectively except in exceptional medical circumstances. Requests for extensions go through your College Tutor; late submission on medical grounds requires a medical certificate. Under no circumstances will work be accepted after it has been marked and returned to other students.

Contacting us

Check the FAQ first — extensions, missed tutorials, readings, mark queries, and GenAI questions are all answered there.

Contact your TA for: anything about module content, readings, tutorials, your discussion points, or how an assignment works goes to the teaching assistant who runs your tutorial group. They are closest to your work and they are happy to meet on request.

Come to me for: extensions, serious personal difficulty, a mark you believe is wrong after you have already discussed it with your TA, or a question about the module as a whole. When you do, copy your TA so we are not answering you separately.

The TAs and I will do our best to reply within two business days. Emails sent at midnight before a deadline will not be answered before it.

For substantive questions about the material, come to office hours (by appointment — email to arrange). I will not be able to fill you in on a lecture you missed, but I am glad to talk through anything you are uncertain about.

Disability

Students with a disability liable to affect their ability to participate fully in this — or any — module are encouraged to register with the University’s Disability Service, which exists to make sure appropriate accommodations are in place.

Keeping up with current events

Read the news! Any serious source you like, national or international. Half the value of this module is having something concrete in your head when a concept turns up, and the other half is noticing that a concept explains something you read last week.

Academic integrity

Do not plagiarise. Academic dishonesty is a serious matter with serious consequences, up to and including no credit for the assignment, failure of the module, and expulsion. It is never permissible to submit work containing other people’s ideas without acknowledgment, and careless note-taking is not an excuse: put direct quotations in quotation marks, and reference paraphrases too. Trinity’s guidance is at libguides.tcd.ie/plagiarism, and if you have not already completed the Library’s Ready, Steady, Write tutorial, do that before your first submission. You must also submit only new work — not work submitted previously for another module.

Generative AI

These tools exist, you likely use them, and you will likely use them in whatever you do after you get your degree. Pretending otherwise would waste both our time. But this module is not assessing whether you can obtain a competent paragraph about political science from an LLM. It is assessing whether you can read an argument, understand it, find its weak and strong points, and put your own case in your own words. That capacity is built by doing the work, and it does not survive being outsourced. So the line I draw is this: if you want to use GenAI (LLMs), use them to help you and to help you improve your prose; do not use them to do the thinking or produce the prose.

What is permitted for each assessment is in that assessment’s sheet, in the vocabulary of the School of Social Sciences and Philosophy Assignment Submission Form — the same words you tick on the form. “Permitted” means you may do it and must tick the corresponding box. “Not permitted” means doing it is an academic integrity matter, whether or not you disclose it.

Familiarise yourself with Trinity’s Statement on Generative AI, the Library’s GenAI guide, and the College GenAI hub.

The Assignment Submission Form

Every piece of written work submitted for this module must have the School of Social Sciences and Philosophy Assignment Submission Form attached as its first page. The form is on Blackboard in both Word and PDF. Complete it in full, including the plagiarism declaration and the GenAI disclosure — tick every category that applies to what you actually did, and use the Other use box if none of them fits.

A written submission received without a completed form will not be accepted. It will be returned to you unmarked, and the work will be treated as not submitted until you resubmit it with a completed form attached — accruing the late penalties set out under Late work above for every day in between. This is a departmental requirement and applies across the module.

The form does not count toward your word limit. Ticking a box that the policy above permits carries no penalty of any kind.

Schedule

Week numbers follow Trinity’s teaching-week calendar (teaching week 1 begins 14 September); as a first-year module, this one starts lectures in teaching week 2. Twenty lectures across ten teaching weeks, in five parts. Readings are indicative; confirm against Blackboard.

Part I — Foundations

Teaching week 2 · Tue 22 & Thu 24 September

Big question: what is politics, and who actually decides?

  • Lecture 1 — Introduction and “Is this politics?” Politics as collective choice under conflict.
  • Lecture 2 — Power: who actually decides? The three faces of power, and how you would ever observe the invisible ones.

Required:

  • Andrew Heywood and Matthew Laing. 2025. Politics. 6th ed. Bloomsbury. Ch. 1.
  • Steven Lukes. 2005. Power: A Radical View. 2nd ed. Palgrave Macmillan. Selected pages.

Recommended:

  • Bernard Crick. 1962. In Defence of Politics. Weidenfeld and Nicolson. Selected pages.
  • Robert A. Dahl. 1957. “The Concept of Power.” Behavioral Science 2(3): 201–215.

Teaching week 3 · Tue 29 September & Thu 1 October

Big question: where do states come from, and why can some of them actually do things?

  • Lecture 3 — The state I: where do states come from? Weber’s monopoly of legitimate violence; war, taxes and bureaucracy.
  • Lecture 4 — The state II: why can some states actually do things? State capacity, and why it is not one number.

Required:

  • Andrew Heywood and Matthew Laing. 2025. Politics. 6th ed. Bloomsbury. Ch. 3.
  • Max Weber. 1946. “Politics as a Vocation.” In From Max Weber: Essays in Sociology, edited by H. H. Gerth and C. Wright Mills. Oxford University Press. Selected pages.

Recommended:

  • Charles Tilly. 1985. “War Making and State Making as Organized Crime.” In Bringing the State Back In, edited by Peter B. Evans, Dietrich Rueschemeyer and Theda Skocpol. Cambridge University Press. Pp. 169–191.

  • Lecture 6 — Ideologies II: nationalism, fascism, feminism, green — and populism as a thin ideology that attaches to any of them.

Required:

  • Andrew Heywood and Matthew Laing. 2025. Politics. 6th ed. Bloomsbury. Ch. 2.

Big question: where do political identities come from, and when does difference become conflict?

Required:
