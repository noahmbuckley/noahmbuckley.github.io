Module Description

This module introduces the big questions in politics and the concepts and tools political scientists use to answer them. Topics run from power and the state through ideologies, democracy and dictatorship, corruption, and institutions to political economy and collective action.

Assessment

Component Weight Details
Discussion points + tutorials 10% Attend all four tutorials; submit discussion points before each.
Short assignment 30% 800-word blog post. Due Friday 23 October 2026, 23:59.
Final essay 60% 1,500-word essay. Due Tuesday 15 December 2026, 23:59.

Full mechanics, deadlines and policies: module outline (syllabus).

Schedule

Week numbers follow Trinity’s teaching-week calendar (teaching week 1 begins 14 September); as a first-year module, this one starts lectures in teaching week 2.

Two lectures per week — Tuesdays at 11am in B101 and Fridays at 1pm in Goldsmith — across ten teaching weeks: teaching weeks 2–6 (22 September – 23 October), then the College reading/review week (teaching week 7, w/c 26 October, no teaching), then teaching weeks 8–12 (3 November – 4 December). Twenty lectures in five parts.

Part I: Foundations

Teaching week 2 — Politics and power

Tue 22 & Fri 25 September

Big question: what is politics, and who actually decides?

Lecture 1: Is this politics? Politics as collective choice under conflict.

Lecture 2: Power — who actually decides? The three faces of power, and how you would ever observe the invisible ones.

Required:

  • Andrew Heywood and Matthew Laing. 2025. Politics. 6th ed. Bloomsbury. Ch. 1.
  • Andrew Heywood and Matthew Laing. 2025. Politics. 6th ed. Bloomsbury. Ch. 4, “Legitimacy and political stability” — Weber’s three types of authority.

Recommended:

  • Peter Bachrach and Morton S. Baratz. 1962. “Two Faces of Power.” American Political Science Review 56(4): 947–952.
  • Steven Lukes. 2005. Power: A Radical View. 2nd ed. Palgrave Macmillan.
  • Bernard Crick. 1962. In Defence of Politics. Weidenfeld and Nicolson. Selected pages.
  • Robert A. Dahl. 1957. “The Concept of Power.” Behavioral Science 2(3): 201–215.

Teaching week 3 — The state

Tue 29 September & Fri 2 October

Big question: where do states come from, and why can some of them actually do things?

Lecture 3: The state I: where do states come from? Weber’s monopoly of legitimate violence; war, taxes, and bureaucracy.

Lecture 4: The state II: why can some states actually do things? State capacity, and why it is not one number.

Required:

  • Andrew Heywood and Matthew Laing. 2025. Politics. 6th ed. Bloomsbury. Ch. 3, including the “Failed states” section.

Recommended:

  • Max Weber. 1946. “Politics as a Vocation.” In From Max Weber: Essays in Sociology, edited by H. H. Gerth and C. Wright Mills. Oxford University Press.
  • Charles Tilly. 1985. “War Making and State Making as Organized Crime.” In Bringing the State Back In, edited by Peter B. Evans, Dietrich Rueschemeyer and Theda Skocpol. Cambridge University Press. Pp. 169–191.
  • Francis Fukuyama. 2013. “What Is Governance?” Governance 26(3): 347–368.

Part II: Ideas & identities

Teaching week 4 — Ideologies

Tue 6 & Fri 9 October

Big question: what are the big answers to who gets what, and who decides?

Lecture 5: Ideologies I: liberalism, conservatism, socialism. Three serious answers to the distribution question.

Lecture 6: Ideologies II: nationalism, fascism, feminism, green — and populism. A thin ideology that attaches to any of them.

Required:

  • Andrew Heywood and Matthew Laing. 2025. Politics. 6th ed. Bloomsbury. Ch. 2.

Recommended:

  • Jan-Werner Müller. 2016. What Is Populism? University of Pennsylvania Press. Ch. 1, “What Populists Say”.
  • Michael Freeden. 2003. Ideology: A Very Short Introduction. Oxford University Press. Ch. 1.

Teaching week 5 — Identity

Tue 13 & Fri 16 October

Big question: where do political identities come from, and when does difference become conflict?

Lecture 7: Where do political identities come from? Why some identities become political and most never do.

Lecture 8: When does difference become conflict? Most diverse societies, most of the time, have no ethnic violence.

Required:

  • Andrew Heywood and Matthew Laing. 2025. Politics. 6th ed. Bloomsbury. Chs. 6 and 8. Selected sections.
  • James D. Fearon. 1999. “What Is Identity (As We Now Use the Word)?” Unpublished manuscript, Department of Political Science, Stanford University.

Recommended:

  • Benedict Anderson. 1991. Imagined Communities: Reflections on the Origin and Spread of Nationalism. Revised ed. Verso. Selected pages.
  • David Yanagizawa-Drott. 2014. “Propaganda and Conflict: Evidence from the Rwandan Genocide.” Quarterly Journal of Economics 129(4): 1947–1994. Introduction only, pp. 1947–1955.

Part III: Regimes

Teaching week 6 — Democracy

Tue 20 & Fri 23 October

Big question: what is democracy, how would you measure it, and why does it survive?

Lecture 9: Democracy: what it is, and how you’d measure it. Minimal against substantive definitions; three indices that disagree.

Lecture 10: Democracy: why it emerges, why it survives.

Required:

  • Andrew Heywood and Matthew Laing. 2025. Politics. 6th ed. Bloomsbury. Ch. 4.
  • Andrew Heywood and Matthew Laing. 2025. Politics. 6th ed. Bloomsbury. Ch. 5, “Classifying political systems” and the regime-types sections.
  • Philippe C. Schmitter and Terry Lynn Karl. 1991. “What Democracy Is… and Is Not.” Journal of Democracy 2(3): 75–88.

Recommended:

  • V-Dem Institute. Democracy Report (latest edition) — overview and key figures.
  • Seymour Martin Lipset. 1959. “Some Social Requisites of Democracy: Economic Development and Political Legitimacy.” American Political Science Review 53(1): 69–105. Selected pages.

Teaching week 7 — reading week (no lectures)

No lectures, no tutorials. (Monday 26 October is a public holiday.) Teaching resumes with teaching week 8 on Tuesday 3 November.

Short assignment due Friday 23 October, 23:59 — the Friday before reading week, via Blackboard

Teaching week 8 — Democracy’s forms, and its absence

Tue 3 & Fri 6 November

Big question: what kind of democracy do we want — and what happens where there isn’t one?

Lecture 11: What kind of democracy do we want? Direct, representative, deliberative; Ireland’s Citizens’ Assembly.

Lecture 12: Dictatorships — how autocracies work. Repression, co-optation, and why hold an election you could cancel.

Required:

  • Andrew Heywood and Matthew Laing. 2025. Politics. 6th ed. Bloomsbury. Ch. 4, “Models of democracy” section.
  • Andrew Heywood and Matthew Laing. 2025. Politics. 6th ed. Bloomsbury. Ch. 5, “Authoritarian regimes” and “Hybrid regimes” sections.
  • Sergei Guriev and Daniel Treisman. 2019. “Informational Autocrats.” Journal of Economic Perspectives 33(4): 100–127.

Recommended:

  • David M. Farrell and Jane Suiter. 2019. Reimagining Democracy: Lessons in Deliberative Democracy from the Irish Front Line. Cornell University Press.
  • Erica Frantz. 2018. Authoritarianism: What Everyone Needs to Know. Oxford University Press. Pp. 1–13.
  • Michael L. Ross. 2001. “Does Oil Hinder Democracy?” World Politics 53(3): 325–361.

Bonus:

  • Tom Pepinsky. 2017. “Everyday Authoritarianism Is Boring and Tolerable.” Blog post.
  • Nic Cheeseman and Brian Klaas. 2018. “How to Steal an Election in Broad Daylight.” Foreign Policy.

Teaching week 9 — When regimes go wrong

Tue 10 & Fri 13 November

Big question: how do democracies rot — and how do you count something everybody hides?

Lecture 13: Democratic backsliding. Not a coup: a slow, legal-looking hollowing-out.

Lecture 14: Corruption and state capture. How you measure a thing people lie about.

Required:

  • Nancy Bermeo. 2016. “On Democratic Backsliding.” Journal of Democracy 27(1): 5–19.
  • Daniel Treisman. 2010. “‘Loans for Shares’ Revisited.” NBER Working Paper 15819. Read for the argument; skip the regression tables.

Recommended:

  • Kim Lane Scheppele. 2018. “Autocratic Legalism.” University of Chicago Law Review 85(2): 545–583.
  • Anna Lührmann and Staffan I. Lindberg. 2019. “A Third Wave of Autocratization Is Here: What Is New About It?” Democratization 26(7): 1095–1113.
  • Anna Persson, Bo Rothstein and Jan Teorell. 2013. “Why Anticorruption Reforms Fail—Systemic Corruption as a Collective Action Problem.” Governance 26(3): 449–471.

Bonus:

  • Civic Forum. 2025. “The State of American Democracy.” Podcast episode with Steven Levitsky, Adam Przeworski, Susan Stokes and Daniel Ziblatt.

Part IV: Institutions & political economy

Teaching week 10 — Institutions

Tue 17 & Fri 20 November

Big question: who can stop whom — and when do rules on paper actually bind rulers?

Lecture 15: What is a parliament actually for? Representation, legislation, oversight — and why the Dáil does less of the second than you would think.

Lecture 16: Rules and referees. Constitutions, courts and veto players, and when paper constraints hold.

Required:

  • Andrew Heywood and Matthew Laing. 2025. Politics. 6th ed. Bloomsbury. Ch. 15, and ch. 14 on executive–legislative relations.
  • Andrew Heywood and Matthew Laing. 2025. Politics. 6th ed. Bloomsbury. Ch. 13.

Recommended:

  • Juan J. Linz. 1990. “The Perils of Presidentialism.” Journal of Democracy 1(1): 51–69.
  • Tom Ginsburg and Aziz Z. Huq. 2018. How to Save a Constitutional Democracy. University of Chicago Press. Chs. 3–4.

Teaching week 11 — Political economy

Tue 24 & Fri 27 November

Big question: who gets what in the economy — and why doesn’t more inequality produce more redistribution?

Lecture 17: Political economy I: states, markets, globalisation.

Lecture 18: Political economy II: inequality, redistribution, housing. Irish housing as a political-economy problem.

Required:

  • Andrew Heywood and Matthew Laing. 2025. Politics. 6th ed. Bloomsbury. Ch. 7.
  • Larry M. Bartels. 2016. Unequal Democracy: The Political Economy of the New Gilded Age. 2nd ed. Princeton University Press. Ch. 1, pp. 1–32.

Recommended:

  • Dani Rodrik. 1997. Has Globalization Gone Too Far? Institute for International Economics. Ch. 1, pp. 1–10.
  • Michelle Norris and Michael Byrne. 2021. “The Political Economy of Housing in Ireland.” In The Oxford Handbook of Irish Politics, edited by David M. Farrell and Niamh Hardiman. Oxford University Press. Pp. 499–518.
  • Ben W. Ansell. 2014. “The Political Economy of Ownership: Housing Markets and the Welfare State.” American Political Science Review 108(2): 383–402.

Part V: Closing

Teaching week 12 — Zooming out

Tue 1 & Fri 4 December

Big question: why do we fail at long-horizon problems — and how would we ever know we were right?

Lecture 19: Climate as a collective action problem. Why democracies under-supply long-horizon goods.

Lecture 20: How do we know? How political scientists get fooled, and how they try not to be.
