Module Description

This module introduces the central questions, concepts, and tools of political science. We start from the basics — what politics is, what power is, why states exist and why some of them work — then take on ideologies and political identity, democracy and dictatorship (and the grey zone between them), corruption, institutions, and who gets what in the economy. Throughout, the goal is not to memorise facts but to learn to think like a political scientist: to ask sharp questions, compare across cases, and use a small toolkit of portable concepts to make sense of a messy political world.

Learning Outcomes

On successful completion of this module, you will be able to:

  1. Apply fundamental concepts and theoretical tools to understand problems in the world of politics
  2. Identify the key questions and problems around which politics revolves
  3. Use core analytical tools — power, the state, democracy, institutions, ideologies — to interpret political events
  4. Compare political arrangements across countries and reason about why they differ

Schedule

Week numbers follow Trinity’s teaching-week calendar (teaching week 1 begins 14 September); as a first-year module, this one starts lectures in teaching week 2.

Two lectures per week — Tuesdays and Thursdays at 11:00 — across ten teaching weeks: teaching weeks 2–6 (22 September – 23 October), then the College reading/review week (teaching week 7, w/c 26 October, no teaching), then teaching weeks 8–12 (3 November – 4 December). Twenty lectures in five parts.

Part I: Foundations

Teaching week 2 — Politics and power

Tue 22 & Thu 24 September

Big question: what is politics, and who actually decides?

Lecture 1: Is this politics? A rent freeze, a nurses’ strike, a viral post, a coup — what makes something political? Politics as collective choice under conflict.

Lecture 2: Power — who actually decides? The three faces of power, and the harder question: how would you ever observe the invisible kinds?

Readings: Heywood & Laing ch. 1; Lukes, Power: A Radical View (selected pages)

Teaching week 3 — The state

Tue 29 September & Thu 1 October

Big question: where do states come from, and why can some of them actually do things?

Lecture 3: Where do states come from? Weber’s monopoly of legitimate violence; war, taxes, and bureaucracy; Hobbes vs Locke.

Lecture 4: Why can some states actually do things? State capacity — why some states collect taxes, deliver vaccines, and hold territory, and others can’t.

Readings: Heywood & Laing ch. 3; Weber, “Politics as a Vocation” (selected pages)

Part II: Ideas & identities

Teaching week 4 — Ideologies

Tue 6 & Thu 8 October

Big question: what are the big answers to who gets what, and who decides?

Lecture 5: Liberalism, conservatism, socialism — three answers to who should get what, and who decides.

Lecture 6: Nationalism, fascism, feminism, green — and populism as a thin ideology that attaches to any of them.

Readings: Heywood & Laing ch. 2

Teaching week 5 — Identity

Tue 13 & Thu 15 October

Big question: where do political identities come from, and when does difference become conflict?

Lecture 7: Where do political identities come from? Why do some identities become political while most never do?

Lecture 8: When does difference become conflict? The real puzzle: most diverse societies, most of the time, have no ethnic violence.

Readings: Heywood & Laing chs. 6 & 8 (selected sections); Fearon, “What Is Identity?”

Part III: Regimes

Teaching week 6 — Democracy

Tue 20 & Thu 22 October

Big question: what is democracy, how would you measure it, and why does it survive?

Lecture 9: Democracy — what it is, and how you’d measure it. Minimal vs substantive definitions; V-Dem, Freedom House, and the grey zone.

Lecture 10: Why democracy emerges, and why it survives.

Readings: Heywood & Laing ch. 4; Schmitter & Karl, “What Democracy Is… and Is Not”

Teaching week 7 — reading week (no lectures)

No lectures, no tutorials. (Monday 26 October is a public holiday.) Catch up on the readings and work on the short assignment.

Teaching resumes with teaching week 8 on Tuesday 3 November.

Short assignment due — [date TBD], via Blackboard

Teaching week 8 — Dictatorships

Tue 3 & Thu 5 November

Big question: how do dictatorships actually work — and why do they bother holding elections?

Lecture 11: How autocracies actually work. Repression vs co-optation.

Lecture 12: Elections, information, succession. Why hold elections you could cancel? Why does nobody tell the autocrat the truth?

Readings: Heywood & Laing ch. 5

Teaching week 9 — When regimes go wrong

Tue 10 & Thu 12 November

Big question: how do democracies rot — and how do you count something everybody hides?

Lecture 13: Democratic backsliding. Not a coup — a slow, legal-looking hollowing-out: courts, media, electoral administration, universities.

Lecture 14: Corruption and state capture. Why “everyone does it” is self-sustaining — and how you count something everybody hides.

Readings: TBD

Part IV: Institutions & political economy

Teaching week 10 — Institutions

Tue 17 & Thu 19 November

Big question: who can stop whom — and when do rules on paper actually bind rulers?

Lecture 15: Who can stop whom? Executives, legislatures, and veto players — one tool you can carry to any country.

Lecture 16: Rules that bind rulers. Constitutions and courts — and when paper constraints actually hold.

Readings: Heywood & Laing chs. 13–15 (selected sections)

Teaching week 11 — Political economy

Tue 24 & Thu 26 November

Big question: who gets what in the economy — and why doesn’t more inequality produce more redistribution?

Lecture 17: States, markets, globalisation.

Lecture 18: Inequality, redistribution — and Irish housing as a political-economy problem, not a policy-detail problem.

Readings: Heywood & Laing ch. 7

Part V: Closing

Teaching week 12 — Zooming out

Tue 1 & Thu 3 December

Big question: why do we fail at long-horizon problems — and how would we ever know we were right?

Lecture 19: Climate as a collective action problem. Why democracies under-supply long-horizon goods.

Lecture 20: How do we know? Political science as detective work — how political scientists get fooled, and how they try not to be.

Readings: TBD

Key Texts

  • Andrew Heywood & Matthew Laing, Politics, 6th ed. (Bloomsbury, 2025) — the course spine. Chapter numbering is unchanged from the 2019 5th edition, so older/second-hand editions are fine.
  • (Optional) Patrick O’Neil, Essentials of Comparative Politics (Norton) — useful on regimes and institutions.
