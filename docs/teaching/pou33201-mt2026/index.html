Module Description

This module introduces the comparative politics of authoritarian regimes: how dictatorships are organised, how they survive, and how they fall. We pair classic and cutting-edge research with contemporary cases from Russia, China, the Middle East, Latin America, and Africa.

Schedule

One two-hour lecture a week, Fridays 14:00–16:00, across twelve teaching weeks — eleven of them with a lecture, since teaching week 7 (w/c 26 October) is reading week. Week numbers below are TCD teaching weeks: teaching week 1 is w/c 14 September, and the first lecture is Friday 18 September. The module is organised by the survival strategies autocrats use to stay in power.

Full details, assessment mechanics and policies are in the module outline.

Part I: Foundations

Teaching Week 1: What Is Authoritarianism? (Friday 18 September 2026)

Topics: Why study dictatorship, and the two questions the module keeps returning to — how autocracies work, and why they last.

Required readings:

  • Erica Frantz. 2018. Authoritarianism: What Everyone Needs to Know. Oxford University Press. pp. 1–13.
  • Sergei Guriev and Daniel Treisman. 2019. “Informational Autocrats.” Journal of Economic Perspectives 33(4): 100–127.

Slides (PDF)

Teaching Week 2: Varieties of Autocracy (Friday 25 September 2026)

Topics: The major types of authoritarian regime, and why classifying them is harder than it looks.

Required readings:

  • Erica Frantz. 2018. Authoritarianism: What Everyone Needs to Know. Oxford University Press. Ch. 5.
  • Steven Levitsky and Lucan A. Way. 2002. “Elections Without Democracy: The Rise of Competitive Authoritarianism.” Journal of Democracy 13(2): 51–65.
  • Milan W. Svolik. 2012. The Politics of Authoritarian Rule. Cambridge University Press. Ch. 1 and 2 (pp. 1–45). This is dense and sometimes technical – focus on the main ideas

Part II: The Dictator’s Dilemmas

Teaching Week 3: How Autocrats Take and Hold Power (Friday 2 October 2026)

Topics: How dictatorships form, and why ruling elites can’t simply trust one another.

Required readings:

  • Barbara Geddes, Joseph Wright and Erica Frantz. 2018. How Dictatorships Work. Cambridge University Press. Chs. 1–2.
  • Milan W. Svolik. 2012. The Politics of Authoritarian Rule. Cambridge University Press. Ch. 3 (pp. 53–63).

Teaching Week 4: The Control Problem — Repression (Friday 9 October 2026)

Topics: The dictator’s dilemma — how much repression is enough, and when it backfires.

Required readings:

  • Christian Davenport. 2007. “State Repression and Political Order.” Annual Review of Political Science 10(1): 1–23.
  • Ronald Wintrobe. 1998. The Political Economy of Dictatorship. Cambridge University Press. Selected pages TBA.
  • Milan W. Svolik. 2012. The Politics of Authoritarian Rule. Cambridge University Press. Ch. 5 (pp. 123–138).

Part III: Survival Strategies

Teaching Week 5: Buying Loyalty — Parties, Parliaments & Patronage (Friday 16 October 2026)

Topics: Why dictatorships build parties and parliaments, and who they buy off to survive.

Required readings:

  • Jennifer Gandhi and Adam Przeworski. 2007. “Authoritarian Institutions and the Survival of Autocrats.” Comparative Political Studies 40(11): 1279–1301.
  • Beatriz Magaloni. 2008. “Credible Power-Sharing and the Longevity of Authoritarian Rule.” Comparative Political Studies 41(4–5): 715–741.

Teaching Week 6: Elections Without Democracy (Friday 23 October 2026)

Topics: Why autocrats hold elections they could just cancel, and what rigging actually buys them.

Required readings:

  • Andreas Schedler. 2002. “Elections Without Democracy: The Menu of Manipulation.” Journal of Democracy 13(2): 36–50.
  • Beatriz Magaloni. 2006. Voting for Autocracy. Cambridge University Press. Selected chapters TBA.

Teaching Week 7: Reading Week (w/c Monday 26 October 2026)

No lecture. Catch up on readings and work on your mid-term essay.

Mid-term essay due Sunday 1 November, 23:59, via Blackboard

Teaching Week 8: Propaganda & Censorship (Friday 6 November 2026)

Topics: Fear dictators versus spin dictators, and why propaganda nobody believes still works.

Required readings:

  • Sergei Guriev and Daniel Treisman. 2022. Spin Dictators. Princeton University Press. Selected chapters TBA.
  • Haifeng Huang. 2015. “Propaganda as Signaling.” Comparative Politics 47(4): 419–444.

Teaching Week 9: Digital Authoritarianism (Friday 13 November 2026)

Topics: What digital surveillance and censorship actually buy a regime, and what AI changes.

Required readings:

  • Sebastian Hellmeier. 2016. “The Dictator’s Digital Toolkit: Explaining Variation in Internet Filtering in Authoritarian Regimes.” Politics & Policy 44(6): 1158–1191.
  • Xu Xu. 2021. “To Repress or to Co-opt? Authoritarian Control in the Age of Digital Surveillance.” American Journal of Political Science 65(2): 309–325.

Part IV: Threats & Breakdown

Teaching Week 10: Coups & the Military (Friday 20 November 2026)

Topics: The coup trap — the people who keep a dictator in power are the ones who can remove him.

Required readings:

  • Sheena Chestnut Greitens. 2016. Dictators and Their Secret Police: Coercive Institutions and State Violence. Cambridge University Press. Ch. 1.
  • James T. Quinlivan. 1999. “Coup-Proofing: Its Practice and Consequences in the Middle East.” International Security 24(2): 131–165.
  • Barbara Geddes, Joseph Wright and Erica Frantz. 2018. How Dictatorships Work. Cambridge University Press. Military/coups chapter.

Teaching Week 11: Protest, Revolution & Breakdown (Friday 27 November 2026)

Topics: Why revolutions catch everyone by surprise, and how autocracies actually end.

Required readings:

  • Timur Kuran. 1991. “Now Out of Never: The Element of Surprise in the East European Revolution of 1989.” World Politics 44(1): 7–48.
  • Barbara Geddes, Joseph Wright and Erica Frantz. 2014. “Autocratic Breakdown and Regime Transitions: A New Data Set.” Perspectives on Politics 12(2): 313–331.

Teaching Week 12: Demise, Democratization & “Autocracy at Home?” (Friday 4 December 2026)

Topics: What comes after breakdown, and the global state of democracy the module leaves you with.

Required readings:

  • Steven Levitsky and Daniel Ziblatt. 2018. How Democracies Die. Crown. Selected chapters TBA.
  • Nancy Bermeo. 2016. “On Democratic Backsliding.” Journal of Democracy 27(1): 5–19.
  • Anna Lührmann and Staffan I. Lindberg. 2019. “A Third Wave of Autocratization Is Here: What Is New About It?” Democratization 26(7): 1095–1113.
