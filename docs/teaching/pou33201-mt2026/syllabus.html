Module description

Why do some rulers govern without meaningful elections, free media, or the rule of law — and why are so many of them so durable? This module introduces the comparative politics of authoritarian regimes: how dictatorships are organised, how they survive, and how they fall.

We examine the major varieties of authoritarian rule — personalist, single-party, military, monarchical, and hybrid or “electoral authoritarian” — and the core strategies autocrats use to stay in power: co-optation, repression, propaganda, elections without democracy, and managing rival elites. Cases are drawn from Russia, China, the Middle East, Latin America, and Africa.

The organising idea runs through every week: a dictator has two problems, not one — controlling the people who put him in power, and controlling everybody else. Nearly everything authoritarian regimes do (parties, parliaments, elections, propaganda, the secret police) is an answer to one of those two problems, and by the end of term you should be able to look at an unfamiliar regime and work out which one it is currently losing.

Learning outcomes

On successful completion of this module, you should be able to:

  • Distinguish between the major types of authoritarian regime and explain how they differ in their institutions and survival strategies.
  • Explain the central dilemmas autocrats face — the problems of power-sharing and of control — and the tools they use to manage them.
  • Evaluate competing theories of authoritarian durability, repression, co-optation, and regime breakdown.
  • Assess how elections, parties, propaganda, and information operate under authoritarian conditions.
  • Apply concepts and research from comparative politics to analyse contemporary authoritarian regimes and the global state of democracy.

How the module works

Lectures

There are eleven lectures, one a week, Fridays 14:00–16:00, across teaching weeks 1–6 and 8–12. Reading week (teaching week 7) has no lecture.

Discussion happens inside the two-hour block: each session mixes presentation with argument, cases, and questions. Come having done the reading.

Attendance is not taken, but material is covered in the room that is not in the readings, and the slides are a prompt for what I say, not a transcript of it.

Slides are posted on the course website after each lecture.

Readings

There is no set textbook. Readings are journal articles and book chapters. Journal articles you find yourself, through the Library or Google Scholar — part of the training. Book chapters and anything else you cannot easily find are provided via Blackboard. Five books recur often enough that you will get to know them:

  • Milan Svolik, The Politics of Authoritarian Rule (Cambridge, 2012)
  • Barbara Geddes, Joseph Wright & Erica Frantz, How Dictatorships Work (Cambridge, 2018)
  • Steven Levitsky & Lucan Way, Competitive Authoritarianism (Cambridge, 2010)
  • Jennifer Gandhi, Political Institutions under Dictatorship (Cambridge, 2008)
  • Sergei Guriev & Daniel Treisman, Spin Dictators (Princeton, 2022)

Each week has required readings to complete before the lecture, plus recommended readings for going deeper. Some weeks also carry a short Bonus list — a blog post, an essay, a podcast episode, a film. Bonus items are not course material: not required, not examinable, and not sources for your written work. The list in the schedule below is indicative; Blackboard and the course website carry the current version.

Assessment

Component Weight
Response papers 10%
Mid-term essay 30%
End-of-term examination (in person) 60%

Full instructions, prompts and essay questions are released in lecture and on Blackboard.

Response papers (10%)

Short pieces of thinking, not summary: take the week’s required readings and do something with them — challenge an argument, apply it to a case the author didn’t use, put two readings into conflict, or connect the reading to something happening now.

  • Length: 500–700 words, +/- 10%.
  • How many: two across the term — one in teaching weeks 1–6, one in teaching weeks 8–12. You choose the weeks.
  • Due: Wednesday 23:59 in the week you choose, via Blackboard.
  • Marking: each paper receives a mark; the two together make up the 10%.
  • The test: could someone who has already done the reading learn something from your response? Restating the argument fails that test; arguing with it passes.

Full instructions: the response paper sheet.

Mid-term essay (30%)

An analytical essay of 2,000 words, 10% either way (excluding bibliography), answering one of several set questions, building an argument out of the academic literature rather than summarising it. Questions are released in lecture and on Blackboard in due course.

  • Due: Sunday 1 November 2026, 23:59.
  • Submission: Word or PDF via Blackboard, which submits to Turnitin.

Examination (60%)

An in-person examination at the end of term, covering the whole module. It rewards the same thing the response papers do: applying a concept from the literature to a case, in your own words, under pressure.

The date is set centrally — check the College examination timetable. Format details will be given later in term.

Written work: formatting requirements

In line with department guidelines, written work for this module must:

  • observe the stated word limit and state the final word count — limits carry a 10% tolerance either way, and going outside that range costs up to 5 marks;
  • be submitted to Blackboard, in Word or PDF format;
  • use proper citation and referencing — any consistent style is acceptable (the TCD Library style guide will help);
  • include an alphabetised bibliography;
  • be spellchecked;
  • have the School of Social Sciences and Philosophy Assignment Submission Form attached as its first page, completed in full (see Academic integrity below).

Back your work up, and submit early enough to survive a technical problem. Keep your submission receipt. Computer crashes, corrupted files, and internet problems are not grounds for late submission.

Policies

Late work

All late work, unless excused in advance by me, is penalised at 5 marks per day or part thereof, up to a maximum of 20 marks. There are many good reasons a student cannot submit on time and I am happy to make reasonable accommodations, but not for requests made after the deadline or a few hours before it.

Requests go through your College Tutor, who contacts me. Extensions are not granted retrospectively except in exceptional medical circumstances, which require a medical certificate. Work is not accepted after it has been marked and returned to other students, or after the end of teaching term — in both cases the mark is 0. It is your responsibility to make sure your College Tutor knows the correct procedure.

Contacting me

Email me at noah.buckley@tcd.ie. Read this document first.

I will do my best to reply within two business days. Email is for logistics: extensions, difficulties, things only I can settle. For substantive questions about the material, come to office hours (by appointment) or raise it in lecture. I will not be able to fill you in on a lecture you missed.

Disability

Students with a disability liable to affect their ability to participate fully in this — or any — module are encouraged to register with the University’s Disability Service, which exists to make sure appropriate accommodations are in place.

Discussion in the room

The lecture is where the arguing happens. People in the room have different backgrounds and personal stakes in these questions — several of the regimes we study are somebody’s home country. Engage seriously with the arguments and generously with the people making them.

Keeping up with current events

Read the news! Any serious source you like, national or international. This is a module about the present tense as much as the past one. Half the value of it is having something concrete in your head when a concept turns up, and the other half is noticing that a concept explains something you read last week.

Academic integrity

Do not plagiarise. Academic dishonesty is a serious matter with serious consequences, up to and including no credit for the assignment, failure of the module, and expulsion. It is never permissible to submit work containing other people’s ideas without acknowledgment, and careless note-taking is not an excuse: put direct quotations in quotation marks, and reference paraphrases too. Trinity’s guidance is at libguides.tcd.ie/plagiarism, and if you have not already completed the Library’s Ready, Steady, Write tutorial, do that before your first submission. You must also submit only new work — not work submitted previously for another module.

Generative AI

These tools exist, you probably use them, and you will almost certainly use them in whatever you do after you get your college degree. But this module is not assessing whether you can obtain a competent paragraph about authoritarian politics from an LLM. It is assessing whether you can read an argument, find its weak point, and put your own case in your own words. So, in general: use these tools to help guide you and to help you fix your prose; do not use them to do your thinking or produce the prose.

What is permitted for each assessment is in that assessment’s sheet, in the vocabulary of the School of Social Sciences and Philosophy Assignment Submission Form — the same words you tick on the form. “Permitted” means you may do it and must tick the box. “Not permitted” means doing it is an academic integrity matter, whether or not you disclose it. The examination is in person and is not covered: nothing to declare, no form.

Trinity offers further resources in the Statement on Generative AI, the Library’s GenAI guide, and the College GenAI hub.

The Assignment Submission Form

Every piece of written work — response papers and the mid-term essay — must have the Assignment Submission Form as its first page. This is a department-wide rule. The form is on Blackboard in Word and PDF. Complete it in full, including the plagiarism declaration and the GenAI disclosure, ticking every category that applies to what you actually did. A submission without a completed form is not accepted — it is returned unmarked and treated as not submitted, accruing late penalties until you resubmit with one. The form does not count toward your word limit, and ticking a permitted box carries no penalty.

I reserve the right to modify this outline as necessary during term. The current version is always the one on the course website and Blackboard.
