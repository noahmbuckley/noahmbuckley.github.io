Off the Hook?

Disasters and Public Opinion in Authoritarian Russia

Noah Buckley

Trinity College Dublin

October 17, 2026

When disaster strikes under autocracy

  • Many autocracies lean on performance to legitimate themselves (Gerschewski 2013)
  • Disasters are tests of that performance
  • The Winter Cherry shopping-centre fire, Kemerovo, 2018
  • Fires in psychiatric hospitals
  • Floods and forest fires
  • So when disaster strikes, does the public hold the regime to account?

Winter Cherry, Kemerovo, 25 March 2018. Photo: Russian Emergencies Ministry (mchs.gov.ru), CC BY 4.0, via Wikimedia Commons.

What we know and what we don’t

  • Democracies: voters punish poor performance (Healy & Malhotra 2009; Gasper & Reeves 2011), even for acts of nature (Achen & Bartels 2016)
  • Autocracies: blame attribution (Beazer & Reuter 2019), affective bonds (Greene & Robertson 2019; Frye 2021)
  • Lazarev et al. (2014): 2010 wildfires raised support, a rally
  • The record is contradictory
  • No multi-event, individual-level test of disaster accountability in an autocracy

Research question

  • Does a disaster in one’s region shift support for the regime (Putin) or its local agent (the governor)?

Russia, briefly

  • Electoral autocracy; media largely state-controlled
  • Putin’s approval is the regime’s currency, polled constantly
  • Governors run the regions, hired and fired by the Kremlin
    • a convenient place to send blame: “good tsar, bad boyars”

Would anyone blame Putin?

  • Scholars expect it: disasters “handled poorly, often spark popular outrage and threaten an autocrat’s hold on power” (Goldring et al. 2025)

  • Russians sometimes aim at the top

    • 2010 wildfires, to Putin’s face: “We asked for help. We trusted you.”
    • Orsk floods, 2024: crowds chanting “Putin, help us!”

  • The Kremlin acts as if blame could reach him: televised dressing-downs of officials; governors as “scapegoats or whipping boys” (Klimovich 2024)

  • Although after the 2010 fires, only 3% named Putin (then prime minister) among officials to fire; governors 15%, fire chiefs 24% (Levada, via Giuliano 2013)

Expectations

  • Accountability: a regime that claims to deliver is judged on delivery; approval falls
  • Insulation or rally: state media, blame pushed down, ideology over performance

Pre-registered:

  • H1 Disasters reduce approval, more so for severe disasters
  • H2 Larger effects on the governor (specific support) than on Putin (diffuse support)

What I find

  • On average, Putin’s approval does not move: any effect is under half a percentage point

    • the exception: the disasters people talk about online

  • Naive “rally” estimates are there before the disaster happens

  • If anyone is blamed, it is the governor: hints, not yet findings

Regime approval is insulated from local disasters, however hard I look

Data and design

Disaster data

  • 4,235 disasters and accidents, 2012–2022, 83 regions
  • Built from FPP regional news digests: ≈95,000 raw events
    • keyword rules + a large language model (Claude) flag disasters
    • the model codes type, deaths, severity, preventability
    • Hand-code samples to verify
  • Deliberately broad: fires, floods, industrial, transport, gas explosions, poisonings, infrastructure failures
  • About a third look like non-disasters (animal die-offs, court cases, announcements): keeping or dropping them gives the same null

What kinds of disasters?

Source: FPP regional digests, 2012–2022.

When exactly?

  • The digests give only the month; a 30-day window needs the day
  • 2,713 events dated to the day:
    • a language model reads news reports for the stated date (1,777)
    • the day social media lit up (701)
    • official records: EM-DAT, Investigative Committee (235)
  • A blind re-check by another model agrees within a day on 94% of its confident answers

And a lot of other data

In the results today

  • EM-DAT, an international disaster database
  • Emergency ministry statistics
  • Regional Telegram and VK channels: how much a disaster is discussed
  • “What events do you remember from the past week?”

Checking the event data

  • Wikipedia and Wikidata
  • Regional newspapers
  • Weather-hazard and air-crash registers: what the digests miss
  • Yandex search volume

In progress

  • Government response: emergency decrees, federal reserve funds, Kremlin and regional-government news, officials fired

Survey data

  • Pooled, harmonized Russian polls: Levada, VTsIOM, FOM
    • 5.4 million responses; 2.6 million (Putin) and 1.2 million (governor) in the disaster years
    • interview date and region for every respondent
    • including VTsIOM daily phone survey, ≈1,600 interviews a day
  • Outcomes (yes / no):
    1. Approval of Putin: diffuse, regime-level support
    2. Approval of the governor: specific, local support

Estimation

Individual-level difference-in-differences

  • Did approval in your region change in the 30 days after a disaster, compared with your region at other times and other regions that month?
  • OLS with region, month, survey fixed effects; SEs clustered by region
  • Polls are not representative within regions, but each region is compared with itself

Key check: a pre-event placebo

A disaster that has not happened yet cannot move approval today

  • So I also estimate the “effect” of a disaster in the next 30 days
  • If a future disaster “predicts” approval as well as a past one, the estimate may reflect which region-months get disasters recorded

Results

Disasters do not move approval

  • Putin: +0.2 points after a disaster (not distinguishable from zero), +0.4 before one
  • Governor: nothing either way

What I expected vs. what I find

Checking the nulls

  • Windows of 7, 14, 30, 60 days
  • Stricter comparisons: within region-year, region trends, seasons
  • Only severe, only serious, only events confirmed by 3+ independent sources
  • Independent disaster records: EM-DAT, emergency ministry
  • Phone vs. face-to-face surveys; pre- vs. post-2014; few-cluster inference
  • The daily phone survey, day by day
  • Other regional news as a placebo: ceremonial news shows the same “before” bump
  • Moderators: salience, novelty, type, respondent characteristics
  • Region by region: for Putin, no more significant regions than chance

No significant drop in Putin’s approval bigger than half a point. The exception: disasters people talk about.

Some cuts: by type of disaster

  • Even disasters the state is plainly on the hook for show no detectable drop

The disasters people talk about

  • Top quarter by attention in regional Telegram and VK channels, 2018–22
  • After: Putin about −0.5 to −0.7 points (p ≈ .05); governor mostly −1 to −2, but noisier and not significant; before: nothing

If anyone is blamed, it’s the governor

  • Big disasters (EM-DAT: 10+ dead, 100+ affected, or an emergency declared)
    • comparing within region-year: governor approval 2.5–4 points lower, one to five months later; flat before
  • Most-discussed disasters online: governor −1.4 (30 days), −2.0 (14 days)
  • But: a handful of big episodes carry it, and in the main model it is weaker. Hints, not findings

Why no accountability for Putin?

  • Tentative reading: the blame channel is severed
    • state control of the information environment
    • legitimation through ideology, not performance
    • blame steered down to governors
  • And most people never hear about a given disaster
    • “what do you remember from the past week?”: even after a 20+ death disaster, only about 1 in 5 locals name it

Can we test these?

  • Information control → compare internet users with non-users; regions with freer media
    • internet users: also nothing
  • Ideology, not performance → before vs. after 2014
    • before 2014, a ~2-point rally; after, no clear effect
  • Nobody hears about it → who remembers which disaster
    • typical disaster: no rise in people naming one

Work in progress: did the state respond?

  • Maybe the average hides rallies when the state responds and punishment when it doesn’t
  • Trying to build this:
    • emergency declared? money paid? regional decrees; federal reserve-fund orders (382, 2012–2023)
    • did leaders show up? Kremlin website; archived regional-government news (62 regions)
    • was anyone punished? officials fired or prosecuted afterwards, from news reports
    • did people notice the response? “what do you remember” answers
  • Status: built from archives and records; not yet checked against hand-coding

Conclusions

  • No measurable accountability in Putin’s approval, 2012–2022: under half a point, severe events included
    • the exception worth chasing: disasters people talk about
  • Naive estimates often mislead: the “rally” is there before the disaster
  • Blame, if any, lands on governors…maybe

Next

  • Who actually knew?
  • Did the state respond? (feasible?)
  • Daily surveys and exactly-dated hazards

Daily phone survey and a stricter comparison

Daily phone survey (VTsIOM, 2018–2022; 1.3 million interviews, 0.46 million of them within a month of 817 dated disasters)

  • Putin approval vs. comparable days with no disaster nearby: +0.8 points in the two weeks before a recorded disaster, +0.2 in the month after (not different from normal days)
  • Before-to-after: −0.6 points (SE 0.2): the “before” bump receding, not a drop below normal. Governor: no change
  • One specification; region-specific 2022 shocks not yet excluded

Stricter comparison (each deadly disaster vs. regions with no disaster within two months)

  • Keeps 19 of 78 events (16 regions): Putin −0.4 (SE 0.5), governor −0.2 (SE 1.5)
  • Too few events: can only detect effects above ~1.4 points (Putin), ~4 (governor)

Day by day, in the daily phone survey

  • VTsIOM daily survey, 2018–2022: 1.3 million interviews, 0.46 million of them within a month of 817 dated disasters
  • Putin: higher in the weeks before; nothing special after. Governor: up on both sides
  • Interview day is imputed (±1–2 days); one specification; 2022 shocks not yet excluded

Other news as a placebo; who notices

Swap disasters for other regional news (same digests, same model)

  • Positive economic news: nothing before (+0.0) or after (−0.1)
  • Positive ceremonial news (sports openings, honours, festivals): +0.5 before, +0.2 after
  • A “before” bump this size is not unique to disasters; it does not say what causes either. Ceremonial items are scheduled in advance

Who notices? (“which events of the past week do you remember?”, VTsIOM 2012–17)

  • Typical disaster: +0.3 points naming a disaster (5% base; not significant)
  • 5+ deaths: +3.7 in the first week; high severity +2.7; about half that by a month

Do positive and negative reactions cancel out?

  • Across regions: for Putin, no more significant regions than chance; the spread is noise
  • Across people: by prior regional support, age, education, internet use, town size: every group within half a point for Putin except low education (+0.8, small subsample, p = .06) (governors: +2 where they were least popular, ~0 elsewhere)
  • Across disasters: each of the biggest disasters sits inside its own placebo range
  • Across eras: a small rally before 2014, no clear effect after
  • Can’t see: opposite reactions among similar people in the same place. A yes/no question only shows the average
  • Next: do disaster-by-disaster effects spread more than placebo “disasters” do?

Severity, deaths, responsibility, confirmation

First recorded disaster in a region

  • Governor −5 points at 30 days with a clean placebo, but a −12-point “before” at 14 days
  • 80 of the 83 “first” disasters fall in the first months of the data (2012–13): mostly a start-of-sample effect
